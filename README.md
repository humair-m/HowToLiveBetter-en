## The Four Resources

This book isn't just about helping you keep more lifespan. There are four things in total:

- **Lifespan**: Live longer, die less from avoidable causes
- **Time and Energy**: Don't spend living hours on zero-return things; let daily attention and physical strength be less drained for nothing
- **Money**: Spend less wasted money; put money where returns are certain
- **Personal Freedom**: Don't end up in detention or a detention center because you didn't know about a red line

Each piece of advice answers two questions: what it costs (money / time / energy / willpower), and what it gets back (change in all-cause mortality / reduction in specific causes of death / time and energy saved / money saved / protection and personal freedom). Entries are sorted by cost-effectiveness, not by category: things that cost almost nothing and yield large benefits go first.

**Benefits fall into different tiers depending on who they land on.** Sorted by "how likely this benefit will come back to you in the future" from high to low: ① **Yourself**; ② **Spouse and immediate family** (parents, children, grandparents, grandchildren); ③ **Friends, colleagues, and other relatives** — reciprocal relationships, help given may come back; ④ **Strangers** — the lowest tier, but not zero: low probability of return, and you don't know their character, plus risks of being extorted, falsely accused, or retaliated against. Different tiers aren't combined; when writing about tier ④, both benefits and risks are listed together.

Read the first-aid section this way: Of 38,227 out-of-hospital cardiac arrests in China, 79.2% happened at home — learning chest compressions is first and foremost for pressing on your own family. Rules like "don't enter the water yourself when you see someone drowning" or "don't physically intervene when you come across a fight" are themselves self-preservation rules, preventing you from turning from bystander into a second casualty. Whether to step in for a stranger is your own trade-off; entries lay out the legal immunities, self-preservation moves, and risk surfaces clearly — they don't write it off as a must-do for you.

Mortality numbers, time/energy numbers, money numbers, and legal consequences are each calculated separately and not converted into each other. These four correspond to the four "what you get back" filters on the search page; they aren't compared in size against one another.

## Evidence Grading

Each piece of advice carries an evidence grade:

| Grade | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| A | Has specific verifiable numbers, sourced from meta-analyses combining multiple studies, large cohorts tracking many people over many years, or randomized controlled trials (RCTs). Can state how much it reduced (HR, RR, percentage decrease) |
| B | Has research support but can't give a precise number; or only small-sample or single-study support |
| C | Author's own experience, or generally accepted practice, with no direct research literature |

Of the 631 entries: 421 are grade A, 159 are grade B, 51 are grade C. An additional 58 are flagged as controversial, and 34 spots are flagged TODO for verification. Controversial A/B-grade entries are marked "controversial" and list counter-evidence. All sources cite only primary literature (journal papers with DOI or PubMed links, or reports from official bodies like WHO, CDC, or the National Bureau of Statistics) — no secondary paraphrasing. Uncertain numbers are marked "to be verified".

## Cost-Effectiveness Tiers

The evidence grade answers only "is this number trustworthy", not "is this thing worth doing". So each entry also carries two additional tags: benefit magnitude (how big the benefit is) and metric (which category of thing is being gotten back). The search page combines these two with three cost items to produce a cost-effectiveness tier:

| Dimension | Values | How It's Determined |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Metric | Lifespan / Money / Time & Energy / Personal Freedom | Based on what the entry primarily gets back. **Different metrics are not compared against each other** — "12% lower all-cause mortality" and "save 500 yuan a year" aren't on the same ruler |
| Benefit magnitude | Large / Medium / Small | Following the entry's own "Benefit" column, fit to predefined thresholds rather than by feel: for lifespan, by percentage reduction (≥20% = large, 10–20% = medium, <10% or only intermediate endpoints measured = small); for money, by amount (ten-thousand-yuan level = large, hundreds to thousands = medium, tens of yuan = small); for personal freedom, by consequence (avoiding criminal liability = large, avoiding detention or administrative penalty = medium, avoiding civil disputes = small); for time & energy, by amount saved (daily hour-level = large, weekly hour-level = medium, one-off saving = small) |
| Cost-effectiveness | Extremely High / High / Average | Large benefit + all three costs zero = Extremely High; large benefit + low cost, or medium benefit + zero cost = High; the rest = Average |

Of the 631 entries: 108 are Extremely High (17%), 289 are High (46%), and 234 are Average (37%). The middle tier being large is intentional: the underlying benefit magnitude only has three levels — Large / Medium / Small — and slicing finer would be fake precision.

**This tier is the author's own judgment, not evidence.** Under this book's own standards, it's only C-grade itself; it's separate from the evidence grade and doesn't influence it. An entry can be A-grade evidence but only Average cost-effectiveness (the shingles vaccine has 97.2% efficacy in a Phase 3 RCT, but two doses cost three to four thousand yuan and shingles is rarely fatal), or C-grade evidence but Extremely High cost-effectiveness (send your itinerary to family before going abroad). "Average" doesn't mean "shouldn't be done" — all entries in the book are recommended; the Average tier just means you have to weigh whether that cost is worth it for you.

## Understanding the Numbers (Glossary)

The main text uses everyday language where possible, but statistical terms inevitably appear when citing research. Look them up here if unclear; on the online search page, hovering over dashed-underlined terms (or tapping on mobile) will also pop up explanations.

<details>
<summary>Expand 41 terms (all-cause mortality, HR, RR, 95% CI, meta-analysis, BMI, LPR, deposit vs. earnest money...)</summary>

| Term | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| All-cause mortality | Over a period of time, the proportion of people in a group who died, regardless of cause. This book uses it to measure "how long one lives". In research it's called ACM |
| HR | Hazard Ratio. Over the same period, the rate at which the group that did something had events (death, illness), divided by the group that didn't. HR 0.87 means 13% lower than the non-doing group; HR 1.21 means 21% higher |
| RR | Relative Risk. The ratio of the probability of an event between two groups. Numbers read the same way as HR |
| OR | Odds Ratio. Also a two-group comparison, but calculated slightly differently from RR: when the event is rare, it's about the same as RR; when common, it exaggerates the gap vs. reality |
| IRR | Incidence Rate Ratio. Incidence is the proportion of new cases of a disease in a period; ratio between two groups, read like RR |
| RaR | Rate Ratio. Compares how many times something happened (e.g., how many falls), not how many people had events; read like RR |
| Standardized Mortality Ratio (SMR) | The actual deaths in this group, divided by "how many people of the same age in the general population would have died". 5.86 means 5.86 times the deaths of same-age peers |
| Risk Difference | The probability difference between two groups, directly yielding "how many more per 1,000 people". Multiples only say how many times; risk difference says concretely how many more people |
| 95% CI | 95% Confidence Interval. Research-calculated numbers always have error; this is the range the true value likely falls within. For multiplicative measures, if the range crosses 1; for additive measures, if it crosses 0 — the difference may be due to chance, and the text will say "no statistical significance" |
| RCT | Randomized Controlled Trial. Split people into two groups by drawing lots; one group does the thing, the other doesn't; compare results afterward. The most persuasive method to prove "this thing caused the effect" |
| Meta-analysis | Combining results from many studies and recalculating for an overall number. Also called a meta-analysis |
| Cohort | Cohort study. Pick a large group of people, track them for many years, record who had events. Can show two things tend to co-occur, but doesn't prove the former caused the latter |
| Observational | Observational study. Researchers only observe and record, without arranging who does what. Results are easily biased by other factors (see "confounding" and "reverse causation" below); numbers should be discounted |
| Confounding | A third factor simultaneously drives both the supposed cause and effect, making two things look causally linked. E.g., people who eat lots of vegetables also tend to exercise more; hard to tell which is doing the work |
| Reverse causation | The causal direction is reversed: looks like A causes B, but actually B causes A. It's not that sleeping more makes people die earlier; it's that severely ill people sleep more |
| d, g | Effect size, expressing how big the gap between two groups is. The gap is converted into "equivalent to how many multiples of typical variation": 0.2 is small, 0.5 medium, 0.8 large |
| r | Correlation coefficient. How tightly two things move together, ranging from -1 to 1. 0.1 weak, 0.3 medium, 0.5 strong |
| MET | Unit of exercise intensity. Sitting quietly is 1 MET; brisk walking is about 3–4 MET. Written as MET·h, it's intensity × hours done — total amount of movement |
| GRADE | An internationally used scoring system rating how reliable a piece of evidence is, in four tiers: high, moderate, low, very low |
| Intention-to-screen analysis | When calculating effect, count by "who was notified to get screened", not by "who actually went". Among those notified, some never go, so this method underestimates the benefit to those who actually go |
| Pack-year | Unit measuring total cigarettes smoked. Packs per day × years smoked. 30 pack-years = 1 pack/day for 30 years |
| BMI | Body Mass Index. A common measure of overweight/underweight: weight in kilograms divided by height in meters squared |
| LDL | Low-density lipoprotein cholesterol, aka "bad cholesterol" |
| eGFR | Estimated Glomerular Filtration Rate. A measure of kidney filtering capacity, in mL/min/1.73 m². Lower means worse kidney function; chronic kidney disease stages are defined by it |
| HBsAg | Hepatitis B surface antigen, a lab test item. Positive means already infected with hepatitis B virus |
| HPV | Human Papillomavirus. Many subtypes; some, when chronically infected, lead to cervical cancer |
| LDCT | Low-dose chest CT, with radiation about 1/5 to 1/10 of a regular CT |
| PM2.5 | Fine particulate matter suspended in air, with diameter under 2.5 micrometers |
| NOVA | A food classification system based on degree of processing, with four categories; "ultra-processed foods" are the fourth |
| LPR | Loan Prime Rate. The benchmark for domestic loan interest rates in China, published on the 20th of each month. Mortgage rates and the upper limit for private lending interest are based on it |
| Final arbitration | Labor arbitration results take effect immediately upon issuance; the employer cannot take the same matter to court |
| Crude marriage rate, Crude divorce rate | The number of marriages or divorces registered in a year per 1,000 people. There's also a "divorce-to-marriage ratio" — divorces in a year divided by marriages in a year — which is different and shouldn't be confused |
| AED | Automated External Defibrillator. The red or yellow emergency boxes common in public places; turn it on and follow voice prompts; it decides itself whether to shock |
| CPR | Cardiopulmonary Resuscitation. Pressing hard on the chest during cardiac arrest to keep blood flowing |
| 3C certification | China Compulsory Certification. Products listed in the catalog can't be manufactured or sold without this mark |
| ICP filing | A registration procedure with the MIIT system that a website or app must complete before officially launching |
| Multi-level protection Scheme | Cybersecurity classified protection system. Levels based on how important a system is; higher levels require more security measures |
| GPL | A type of open-source license. Things built using such code usually must also be released with their own source code open |
