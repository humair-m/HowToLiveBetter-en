# How the Body Tells Time, and Why Night Shifts Hurt

This is the long-form version of Section 2, Entry 40 (the longer you work night shifts, the higher your cardiovascular risk). The entry itself only does the accounting; this piece explains the underlying reasoning.

The question to answer is: can the body really tell what time it is? And if it can, why is working night shifts not something you "get used to after a few times"?

## 1. The body really does have a timekeeping organ

In the hypothalamus of the human brain sits a small cluster of neurons called the suprachiasmatic nucleus, about twenty thousand cells. It is the body's master clock.

It keeps time through a set of "clock genes" that mutually inhibit one another. Two proteins called CLOCK and BMAL1 switch on the transcription of two other genes, PER and CRY. Once the PER and CRY proteins accumulate, they turn back and shut off the pair that switched them on. When these proteins have degraded, the cycle begins again. One revolution takes about 24 hours.

So this clock does not rely on observing the outside world to infer time. Put a person in a room with no light, no clock, and no social cues, and the rhythm still keeps running. It is just that the period is not exactly 24 hours; it drifts a little day by day. The 2017 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine was awarded to the three researchers who discovered this molecular mechanism.

## 2. This clock sets itself by light, but not via the "seeing things" circuit

On the retina, in addition to the cells responsible for forming images, there is a dedicated class of light-sensing cells. They contain a pigment called melanopsin; they do not participate in seeing clearly, they only send the intensity of light straight to the suprachiasmatic nucleus.

So whether light can calibrate the biological clock is not directly related to whether the eyes can see. Some completely blind people who cannot see anything still have their biological clocks calibrated by light.

This is also why Section 3, Entry 2 (a fixed wake-up time, including on weekends) is paired with getting light in the morning.

## 3. The key point: there is more than one clock

The liver, the gut, the heart, and adipose tissue each have their own peripheral clock.

The master clock is calibrated by light. **The peripheral clocks are calibrated largely by "when you eat."**

Under a normal schedule, these two are in sync. You see light during the day and eat during the day; the master clock and the peripheral clocks point to the same time. So normally you do not even notice that they are two separate sets of things.

## 4. The problem with night shifts is not "staying up," it is that the two clocks are pulled apart

People who work night shifts still see light during the day, and they still live a daytime social life. So the master clock remains essentially locked to the external day-night cycle.

But you eat, work, and mobilize metabolism during the biological deep night. The peripheral clocks are pulled toward the other side.

This state is called circadian misalignment. The problem is not that you have slept a few hours less, but that the clocks throughout the body cannot align with one another.

## 5. And you cannot adapt

Some might think, "long-term fixed night shifts, surely I'll adapt."

One review pooled six studies that used the melatonin secretion rhythm to measure long-term fixed night-shift workers. The melatonin rhythm is the most reliable indicator of the state of the endogenous biological clock.

The result: fewer than 3% achieved "complete" adaptation. Fewer than a quarter achieved "a considerable degree" of adaptation.

How many years they had been doing it made no difference. The reason sits in the previous paragraph: you still see light during the day, and you still live a daytime social life; these cues keep pulling the master clock back.

Source: Folkard S (2008). Do permanent night workers show circadian adjustment? A review based on the endogenous melatonin rhythm. Chronobiology International. <https://doi.org/10.1080/07420520802106835>

## 6. How do we know it is the misalignment itself that is harming, not just losing sleep

This is the most worthwhile layer of the whole matter to ask about. Night-shift workers sleep less, eat erratically, are under more stress, and smoke more — any of these could be the real cause.

Fortunately, this can be done in isolation in a laboratory.

Scheer et al. 2009 study: 10 adults stayed in a laboratory for 10 days. The investigators artificially set "a day" to 28 hours, forcing their eating and sleeping to cycle through every moment of the biological clock. **Four meals a day, with calories fixed.**

When behavior and the biological clock were about 12 hours out of alignment, the results were:

- Leptin dropped 17% (leptin is the hormone that makes you feel full)
- Blood glucose rose 6%, and this was on top of insulin having already risen 22%
- The daily cortisol rhythm completely inverted
- Mean arterial pressure rose 3%
- Sleep efficiency dropped 20%

The most striking result: of the 8 people with sufficient data, 3 had post-meal blood glucose entering the prediabetic range, after only 10 days.

Because the calories were fixed and the experiment was controlled in a laboratory, these changes cannot be blamed on "less sleep" or "overeating." **Misalignment alone was sufficient to bring about these changes.**

Source: Scheer FA, Hilton MF, Mantzoros CS, Shea SA (2009). Adverse metabolic and cardiovascular consequences of circadian misalignment. PNAS, 106(11), 4453-4458. <https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.0808180106>

## 7. The first thing you can change: move eating into the daytime

Since the peripheral clocks are calibrated by eating time, there is one direction worth trying: keep working the night shift, but do not eat at night.

One randomized controlled trial did this (registration NCT02291952). 20 healthy adults simulated night-shift work and were randomized into two groups. One group followed the typical habit of night-shift workers, eating both at night and during the day. The other group ate only during the day.

The primary-outcome paper reported: in the group that ate at night, the central rhythm and the peripheral glucose rhythm fell out of alignment, and glucose tolerance worsened; the daytime-only eating group did not show either of these.

The cardiovascular paper (a secondary analysis of the same trial) reported: in the group that ate at night, the cardiac vagal modulation index pNN50 dropped 25.7%, RMSSD dropped 14.3%, the LF/HF ratio reflecting sympathetic-vagal balance rose 5.5%, and the prothrombotic factor PAI-1 rose 23.9%. These changes did not appear in the daytime-only eating group.

**But this is only 20 people, and it was simulated night-shift work, not real night-shift workers.**

A systematic review specifically looking for such randomized trials searched six databases and found only 4 trials. The results were not uniform: some trials saw improvements in fasting insulin and insulin resistance, some did not; the high-density lipoprotein result showed no change in any of the four trials; the post-meal glucose result did show that a 10.75-hour night-time fast was better than eating both dinner and a night snack. The review authors themselves said that the trials are too few to be generalized.

So what can be said now is only this much: **concentrating eating during the daytime on a night-shift schedule has support from randomized trials, but the evidence is still thin.** It costs nothing and is not hard to do; if you want to try it, try it. There is a second thing to try, in the next section: using light to move the entire biological clock over.

Source: Chellappa SL et al. (2021). Daytime eating prevents internal circadian misalignment and glucose intolerance in night work. Science Advances. <https://doi.org/10.1126/sciadv.abg9910> ; Chellappa SL et al. (2025). Daytime eating during simulated night work mitigates changes in cardiovascular risk factors: secondary analyses of a randomized controlled trial. Nature Communications. <https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-025-57846-y> ; Wulandari F et al. (2026). Cardiometabolic status among shift workers under meal time regulation: A systematic review of randomized controlled trials. Nutrition and Health. <https://doi.org/10.1177/02601060261464874>

## 8. Can the clock be moved over

Everything above is about "misalignment being harmful." In reverse, can the clock be moved entirely onto the night-shift side?

**In theory yes, and it has been done. But the requirement for light is an order of magnitude higher than most people imagine.**

Czeisler et al. 1990 study: 8 young men worked a week of night shifts, comparing two conditions.

In the control condition, the night had ordinary indoor lighting, about 150 lux. Lux is the unit of illuminance; an ordinary fluorescent-lit room is roughly one to two hundred. The result was that over six consecutive nights, the core body temperature minimum still fell around 3:30 a.m. The core body temperature minimum is the standard indicator for judging where the biological clock points. This means the biological clock did not budge.

In the treatment condition, bright light of 7,000 to 12,000 lux was given at night, and daytime sleep took place in a nearly pitch-black environment. After four days, the body temperature minimum moved to near 3:00 p.m. The entire clock had been flipped.

Source: Czeisler CA, Johnson MP, Duffy JF, Brown EN, Ronda JM, Kronauer RE (1990). Exposure to bright light and darkness to treat physiologic maladaptation to night work. New England Journal of Medicine, 322(18), 1253-1259. <https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJM199005033221801>

### Three things, none can be missing

**First, the light at night must be bright enough.** Going to work under ordinary fluorescent lighting is equivalent to the "nothing happened" control group above. For it to work, you need a dedicated light-therapy light box.

**Second, daytime sleep must be strictly light-blocked.** Blackout curtains plus an eye mask. The experiment above did "bright light at night" and "near pitch-black during the day" together — not one or the other.

**Third, wear sunglasses on the way home from work.** This is the easiest to miss, and missing it essentially undoes everything.

Why the third matters so much has to do with how light acts: the same light, falling at different times, pushes the biological clock in opposite directions. Light exposure **before** the body temperature minimum pushes the clock later; light exposure **after** it pulls the clock earlier.

When you walk home after a night shift, you are exactly past the body temperature minimum. **What this morning light does is in the opposite direction from what sitting in front of a light box all night does.**

### Done in the lab, not in real life

The few people in the lab did not step outside for a week. In reality, on your days off you live a daytime life — picking up the kids, spending time with family, running errands. Do that once, and the clock gets pulled back. This is where the figure in Section 5 comes from: fewer than 3% of long-term fixed night-shift workers can fully adapt.

So in the real world, the goal is typically not a complete flip, but only a partial move, parking the clock in the middle position where "you are alert enough at work and your days off are not entirely wrecked."

### How much can be had in reality

A meta-analysis pooling 11 studies: light therapy gave shift workers about 32.5 minutes more total sleep time, and improved sleep efficiency by about 2.91. The effective dose was 900 to 6,000 lux, at least 1 hour per night.

Another laboratory study used intermittent bright light. In the treatment group, melatonin secretion overlapped with the scheduled sleep period for 4.90 hours; in the control group, only 2.62 hours (P=0.002). The cognitive decline that the control group showed on the last night shift was held down in the treatment group. The authors explicitly note in their conclusion that this approach still needs to be verified in real night-shift workers.

Source: Zhao C, Li N, Miao W, He Y, Lin Y (2025). A systematic review and meta-analysis on light therapy for sleep disorders in shift workers. Scientific Reports. <https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-024-83789-3> ; Lammers-van der Holst HM et al. (2021). Efficacy of intermittent exposure to bright light for treating maladaptation to night work on a counterclockwise shift work rotation. Scandinavian Journal of Work, Environment & Health. <https://doi.org/10.5271/sjweh.3953>

### One thing nobody has measured

"If the clock is moved over correctly, will cardiovascular risk fall along with it?" — **no trial has measured this**.

What is known is that misalignment is harmful; that was established by the experiment in Section 6. But that does not mean the reverse, that "moving the clock over correctly is safe." And shifting the clock back and forth on rest days — whether that back-and-forth is good or bad — has likewise not been measured.

The endpoints of the two countermeasures above — one to metabolic and cardiovascular indicators, one to sleep — have not been taken all the way to disease and death.
