## A judgment overturned by the user (recorded here)

In my first report I recommended writing only the catch-up sleep entry, not the night-shift one, on the grounds that night shift "has only risks and no path", invoking the 2026-09-18 "only-prohibition-no-path" screening criterion. The user pushed back: "but I think the reader can choose whether to work nights or not; some might switch jobs for their health, which is better than nothing". The pushback holds; my judgment was overapplying the rule: switching jobs, applying for a transfer, and weighing the cost when choosing a job are all real choices, and the dose-response from 23 cohort studies (+7% per additional 5 years) is exactly the quantitative basis for "switch early". The book already has the "lay out the costs only, no conclusion" approach (Section 10 on marriage, Section 18 on raising a child); night shift can be written the same way. **Lesson: the "no path" judgment must first confirm whether the person really has no choice, not treat a high cost of choice as no choice.**

## What was not written: IARC 2A

The widely-circulated "WHO classifies night shift as a 2A carcinogen" **was not written into the entry**. The IARC official site (the classification table on monographs.iarc.who.int, and publications.iarc.who.int/593) are all front-end dynamically loaded; this machine scraped only an empty shell, with no body text available; the official working group's published version is Ward EM et al. Per "if a number is uncertain, it's better not to write it than to make it up from memory", two cancer-epidemiology papers that can be verified word-for-word are used instead, and they give conclusions that are much weaker than the circulating claim, so the body states "the evidence is much softer than the circulating claim". ## Grading and selection

- **Entry 40 graded B**: Only one cohort (plus one NHANES replication), observational. Benefit set to "medium": the gap between 1.42 and non-significance is large enough, but all are associations, and "the rebound group is non-significant" means no difference was detected, with point estimates still above 1; the note states this.
- **Entry 41 graded A**: A dose-response meta-analysis of 23 cohort studies, numbers verifiable word-for-word. Benefit set to "large": cardiovascular mortality +27%, mechanically applied under the mortality metric ≥20% threshold.
- **Entry 41 cost tag filled at the harder tier** (money=many time=medium willpower=yes): transferring or switching jobs means less money, no night-shift differential. The cost column also states the "weigh the cost when choosing a job, cost is zero" tier, but the tag can only be one set; filled high not low.
- **A self-contradiction in the body was handled**: Entry 40 says "make up sleep after staying up late"; Section 3, Entry 2 says "wake up at the same time on weekends"; the social-jet-lag paper says a weekday–weekend sleep-midpoint difference ≥2 hours raises risk by 30%. Verification of the original text confirmed the three are not in conflict—the catch-up night in that study is "the first night after a short-sleep night" with each segment counting only one night, not a weekend bulk catch-up. The Entry 40 title was written as "make up sleep the next night, don't save it up for the weekend"; both the note and the Section 3, Entry 2 note state: the method is to go to bed earlier that same night, not to sleep in the next day until noon.
- **Social jet lag is not a separate entry**: it overlaps heavily with Section 2, Entry 13's "sleep regularity predicts mortality better than sleep duration", so it was added to that entry's benefit column, and the note's "only one follow-up study supports" was changed to "two".

### Selection choices

- **This path carries more weight than the "eat during the day" one**: it directly measures the phase of the body clock itself (lowest body temperature, melatonin rhythm), not a surrogate endpoint, and the 150-lux control group's "six nights, no shift" answers exactly the user's question about whether daylight lamps work—they don't, by an order of magnitude.
- **The three prerequisites are written as hard conditions in both body and long-form**: bright-enough light at night, strict daytime blackout, and sunglasses on the commute home. The reason for the third is the direction of the light phase-response: light before the body-temperature minimum pushes the clock later, light after pulls it earlier, and the commute home is after, so morning light cancels the whole night's light box. Skipping it undoes everything.
- **Effect magnitude written honestly to where it reaches**: the meta-analysis's endpoints are only total sleep time (+32.54 minutes) and sleep efficiency (+2.91), not morbidity and mortality. The 2021 paper is a lab simulation; the authors themselves request verification in real night-shift workers; both body and long-form state this.
- **Explicitly states "no one has tested whether shifting the clock correctly lowers cardiovascular risk"**: what is known is that misalignment is harmful (Scheer 2009), which does not imply the reverse holds; whether back-and-forth shifting on rest days helps or harms is also unmeasured. The long-form has a dedicated subsection on this.
- **The note length was handled**: after adding, Entry 41's note briefly reached 998 characters, becoming the longest in the book (the median across all 600+ notes is about 130 characters, 90th percentile about 273). Per CLAUDE.md "long explanations go in docs/", the dosing, raw numbers, and additional sources were moved into the long-form; the note kept only the decisive comparison (150 lux six nights no shift vs 7000–12000 lux four days shifted), the three hard conditions, and two limitations, brought under 998 characters and no longer the longest. **The longest note in the book is now Section 6, Entry 26 (breakfast, 1127 characters), also written this round, not yet handled.**
- **Two self-contradictions in the long-form caused by the addition were fixed**: section seven's heading was "the one thing you can change" (now two things; changed to "the first thing"), and the closing originally read "is currently the only countermeasure supported by an RCT" (removed, and points to the next section).
