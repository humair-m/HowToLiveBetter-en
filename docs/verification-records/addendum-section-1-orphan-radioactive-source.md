# Section 1 Addendum: Don't Pick Up or Disassemble Unknown Metal Objects (2026-09-19)

Task origin: A user noted the book lacks a piece on "things that glow or heat, unknown metal, possibly nuclear leakage", and raised "dismantling an X-ray machine for scrap, dismantling something with radiation" and "there are many cases online", while also asking how likely an ordinary person is to encounter this.

Existing coverage: None. A grep for "radiation source (放射源) / radiation (辐射) / radioactive (放射性) / nuclear leakage (核泄漏)" found only three unrelated hits—Section 6 PET-CT entry on the ionizing radiation from the examination itself; Section 8 mention of "radioactive" in the list of crimes under Criminal Law Article 114; Section 28 on the prohibition of selling radioactive drugs online. Orphan radioactive sources, radioactive sources in scrap metal, and disassembly of source-containing equipment were nowhere.

Landing: Section 1 adds 1 new entry, numbered Entry 34 at the end of the section, without renumbering any existing entry or causing any cross-section reference changes. Placed at the end rather than inserted at the front by "zero cost, large benefit"—see the ordering note below.

## What was not written into the body, and why

**The Nanjing "5·7" iridium-192 loss incident (2014) is not cited.** The facts fit the "passerby pick-up" scenario best—Tianjin Hongdi Company lost a Category II iridium-192 source during radiography work at the Sinopec Fifth Construction Company yard; two workers on their way to work saw "something that looked like a necklace", picked it up, looked at it, and dropped it; the source was uncontrolled for nearly 40 hours; recovered on May 10 in a marshy grassy area about 2 km away; the Nuclear Industry General Hospital treated the exposed personnel. However, this round found no verifiable incident notice or investigation report on the websites of the Ministry of Ecology and Environment, the National Nuclear Safety Administration, or the China Atomic Energy Authority; the retrieved details are scattered across local ecological-environment department work updates and political advisory figures, which are retellings; the "picker exposure dose approximately 1.3 Gy" appearing in search results likewise has no official source. The whole case is therefore not written into the body. The Goiânia case itself is the situation of a non-professional extracting a source from abandoned equipment, and has complete IAEA data, sufficient to play the same role.

**"Cesium salts glow blue in the dark" is not written.** This is the source of the user's "glow or heat" question, widely circulated, but the IAEA material verified this round did not contain this statement word-for-word. The body uses this in reverse: explicitly stating "most radioactive sources are just a piece of ordinary metal, neither glowing nor emitting heat", plugging the "judge by senses" misconception head-on—which is more useful than retelling glow details and does not require that unverified description.

**12369 is not written.** Drafting originally intended to write "call 110 and 12369", but verification found that the "12369 environmental reporting hotline" short number was fully retired in 2024 and is now handled by the National Ecological-Environment Petition and Complaint Reporting Management Platform. The body only writes "call 110" and per Article 42 of the Regulations "report to the ecological-environment, public security, and health authorities", without writing the retired number.

**"Does an X-ray machine have radiation after being dismantled?"** The user's example mixes in a common misconception. Article 68 of the Regulations defines "radiation devices" as "X-ray machines, accelerators, neutron generators, and devices containing radioactive sources"—the same category includes both X-ray machines that produce radiation only when powered and have no residual radiation when off, and devices that themselves contain a sealed source. So the note does not blanket-state "medical equipment is dangerous" or "X-ray machines are fine"; instead, it draws the line: the danger lies in whether the device contains a sealed radioactive source (radiotherapy machines, gamma radiography machines, well-logging instruments, level gauges contain sources), and gives an actionable rule—ordinary people cannot distinguish by appearance, so unknown device parts should never be disassembled or sold.

## Ordering note

Section 1 is roughly ordered by cost-effectiveness, but this entry, despite all three cost items being zero and benefit magnitude being "large", has a very low baseline incidence rate: per the bulletin caliber, fewer than 1 accident per 10,000 sources per year, combined with about 178,000 sources in use, puts the national annual radiation-accident count at the level of a dozen or so, and most occur in professional settings such as radiography, medical use, and industrial measurement—not falling on ordinary people. Inserting it at the front would crowd out the high-frequency entries on traffic, gas, and fire, contradicting the "ordered by cost-effectiveness" intent—expected benefit must be multiplied by incidence rate. So it is numbered Entry 34 at the end of the section. The body's plain-language sentence states honestly "the probability of an ordinary person encountering this is extremely low", not using case density to manufacture panic; the entry's justification is that cost is zero and consequences are irreversible, not that probability is high.
