# Addendum: Breakfast and Eating Time Entry · Verification Record (2026-09-21)

Task origin: The user asked "is there content on whether to eat breakfast, and whether to eat on schedule or to eat when hungry".

Existing coverage: Whole-book search for "breakfast (早饭)", "breakfast (早餐)", "three meals", "eat on schedule", "fasting (断食)", "fasting (禁食)", "eating window", "time-restricted (限时)" returned only Section 28, Entry 1 (don't use extreme dieting, fasting, or vomiting to control weight), which is about eating disorders and does not answer "whether to eat breakfast or at what time". The two "three meals" mentions in Section 29 are about someone watching that the bereaved eats. This topic: zero coverage.

Landing: Section 6, Entry 26, appended at the end of the section. It is in the negative list because what is being rejected is two popular claims ("eating breakfast helps health and weight control" and "16:8 time-restricted eating is more effective than simply eating less"), not the act of eating itself. The two claims are merged into one entry because they are two faces of the same error: both assume that "when you eat" can have an effect independent of "how much you eat"; splitting them into two entries would let the reader see only half and pivot to "then let me try 16:8". The metric is mortality (health endpoint and body weight).

## Grading and selection

- **2026-09-21 same-day rework (user pointed out two issues)**: The first draft's title was "Don't force yourself to eat breakfast for health, and don't count on 16:8 time-restricted eating being more effective than simply eating less"; the plain-language opening was "the claim that eating breakfast helps weight control is reversed". The user's exact words were "to a normal person, that breakfast-eaters weigh more is normal; whether long-term skipping of breakfast causes harm is also not addressed". Both points hold: ① The target was lost. "Breakfast-eaters weigh more" is not counterintuitive to readers—eating an extra meal obviously increases intake. What the BMJ piece actually refutes is the popular claim that skipping breakfast leads to overeating at lunch and dinner and a higher total daily intake; the key number is that the skip-breakfast group's total daily intake was lower by 259.79 kcal, i.e., not compensated back. The plain-language and benefit column were rewritten to set this target first, and the intake item was moved ahead of weight. ② "Whether long-term breakfast skipping causes harm" is the reader's core question; the first draft placed it entirely in the notes, but per the book's rules the plain-language section may only use content from the benefit column, so it could not be written in the most prominent position. The two observational studies (Zhang 2025, Rong 2019) were moved from the notes into the benefit column and source column; the note retains only the confounding-explanation. The title was simultaneously changed to "Don't count on breakfast or 16:8 time-restricted eating to control your weight; pick an eating time you can sustain long-term"; the cross-reference anchor in Section 2, Entry 34 was updated accordingly. The benefit column thus became a "three-stage" structure: first refute the popular claim, then look at 16:8, then answer the long-term-harm question.
- **2026-09-21 second rework (user raised two folk claims)**: The user's exact words were "I read online that skipping breakfast makes your intestines digest feces, equivalent to eating feces; others say it's bad for the stomach". Verification results: ① "Intestines digesting feces" has no corresponding mechanism in the literature. The colon's reabsorption of water and electrolytes is a constant normal physiology, unrelated to breakfast; feces are not absorbed as food. This claim is not written into the body—the book does not write "myth-busting" entries (per the writing rules: entries are written for the reader, not to respond to the question asker); stating positively what to check is enough. ② "Bad for the stomach" is half true and half false; the true half is not the stomach but the gallbladder, which was added to the notes (see the two papers in the table below). At the same time, the original note's sentence "Looking at both together, the effect of eating time is very small; don't spend energy on it" was judged as overstatement: three independent lines of observational evidence—cardiovascular, gallstones, digestive-tract cancer—point in the same direction; although none can prove causation, it should not be covered by a single "very small effect". Changed to separate statements—for weight control, the effect is small; for whether long-term complete breakfast skipping is harmful, there is only correlational evidence, directionally consistent and leaning unfavorable, so for safety eat normally, just don't count on it for weight loss. The "but total mortality shows no difference" in the plain-language final sentence was changed to "Total mortality shows no difference", dropping the "but" that reads as reassurance.
- **Two papers added this round (both placed in the notes, not the benefit column)**:
  - <https://doi.org/10.3389/fnut.2024.1521707> (Sun T, Zhang L, Lu Y, et al. Front Nutr 2024;11:1521707, PMID 39737153). Original text: "A total of 6,547 participants were included. The fully adjusted model indicated a positive correlation between the FMT of the day and the prevalence of gallstones (odds ratio [OR] = 1.05, 95% confidence interval [CI] = 1.02–1.08)"; "The risk of developing gallstones was the greatest when the FMT was between 09:00 and 14:00 (OR [95% CI] = 1.49 [1.24–1.77])". FMT = first meal time. **It is a cross-sectional survey, cannot determine causation direction**; the note states that pre-existing gallbladder issues may have changed eating habits.
  - <https://doi.org/10.1007/s11606-023-08094-7> (Liu T, Wang Y, Wang X, et al. J Gen Intern Med 2023, PMID 36869181, Kailuan cohort ChiCTR-TNRC-11001489). Original text: "During a median follow-up of 5.61 (5.18–6.08) years, 369 incident GI cancer cases were identified."; "Participants who did not eat breakfast had an elevated risk of esophageal (HR = 2.72, 95% CI: 1.05-7.03), colorectal (HR = 2.32, 95% CI: 1.34-4.01), liver (HR = 2.41, 95% CI: 1.23-4.71), gallbladder, and extrahepatic bile duct cancer (HR = 5.43, 95% CI: 1.34-21.93)." The note cites only the colorectal and gallbladder/bile-duct items, **and reproduces the 1.34–21.93 interval verbatim**, letting the reader see that there were too few events.
- **Checked but not written in**: <https://doi.org/10.1186/s41043-025-01223-4> (Ostadsharif N et al., J Health Popul Nutr 2026, PMID 41527009), a meta-analysis of 11 studies and 21,220 people, "higher meal frequency was associated with a reduced risk of FD (n = 2, OR = 0.52, 95% CI: 0.32-0.86)". Only 2 studies support that item, and FD (functional dyspepsia) is a symptom diagnosis with high likelihood of bidirectional causation with "meal frequency"; the evidence is too thin, not adopted.
- **Graded A**: The three primary pieces of evidence in the body are all randomized controlled trials (a meta-analysis of 13 trials + two RCTs, one of which followed up for 12 months), numbers verifiable word-for-word.
- **Benefit "small"**: This entry rejects two ineffective practices; the reader who follows through gets zero, not a reduction; per the "surrogate endpoints only = small" threshold applied. All three cost items are zero; lands in the average cost-effectiveness tier (pulled down by small benefit).
- **Marked "disputed", with primary literature on both sides**: The observational side (skipping breakfast raises cardiovascular disease by 17%, cardiovascular mortality by 49%) and the time-restricted-eating side (still loses an extra 1.46 kg under isocaloric conditions) both contradict the body's conclusion; each is written into the notes with its DOI.
- **Explicitly notes that NHANES III all-cause mortality is non-significant**: HR 1.19 (0.99–1.42) crosses 1. Online retellings of this study typically cite only the cardiovascular-mortality 1.87 and omit the all-cause item, so the note places the two numbers side by side.
- **How to reconcile the two sides of time-restricted eating**: The meta-analysis (30 studies, mostly short-term) says there is an additional benefit; the longest and strictest single trial (NEJM 12 months) says there is none. The note is written as "the timing of meals has a small effect", not as "completely ineffective".
- **Not written**: mechanistic inferences about eating time and circadian rhythm (the so-called chrononutrition, where eating early is better than eating late), insufficient human-endpoint evidence, not adopted; nor was any claim that "fasting extends lifespan" written—there is no RCT with a mortality endpoint in humans.
- **Applicability scope added**: People with diabetes, those on glucose-lowering medications, and pregnant women may go hypoglycemic if they skip a meal; the note states this exclusion and defers to the physician's instructions.
