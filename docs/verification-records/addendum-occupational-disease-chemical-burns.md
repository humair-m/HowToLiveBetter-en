# Addendum: Occupational Disease Prevention, Chemical Burns, and Blue-Light Filtering · Verification Record (2026-09-08)

Task origin: The reader raised three things — "Is there anything on chemical burn treatment, like getting acid on you?"; "Some factories in China have no safety measures at all, with many accidents and dust, causing sequelae over decades"; "And is looking at screens harmful? They're all intense light, after three months you basically can't see clearly."

Existing coverage: Chemical burns are only half-covered — Section 13, Entry 12 (added last round) wrote about flushing skin and eyes for 15 minutes, but the whole entry is written around "household cleaners and accidental ingestion," without workplace, decontamination of clothing, safety data sheets, or employer responsibility; Section 13, Entry 6 on burns only covers thermal burns. Occupational disease is a complete blank: searching the whole book for "occupational disease" only turned up hits inside Section 19's work-injury regulation quotation and one TODO in Section 23, with "pneumoconiosis," "dust," "noise," "goggles" returning zero hits. Screens and eyes are also a complete blank: "visual fatigue," "dry eye," "blue light," "myopia" all zero hits.

**The reader's third claim needs to be corrected in the body**: Ordinary screens will not cause someone to "basically not be able to see clearly after three months." What screens cause is digital visual fatigue and dry eye, which are reversible; the intense light that causes irreversible eye damage in the short term is welding arc light, ultraviolet, and laser, which are occupational hazards calling for protective goggles, not blue-light-filtering lenses. So this entry is written as "which kinds of light actually damage eyes and which don't," landing on the negative list.

Landing point: Section 13 gets one new entry (Entry 13, with original Entries 13–28 renumbered to 14–29, and the reference in docs/家庭应急装备清单.md pointing to Section 13, Entry 16 changed to Entry 17); Section 19 gets two new entries (Entries 10 and 11, inserted before the work-injury block, with original Entries 10–14 renumbered to 12–16, and the section-opening segmentation note changed to "the last seven entries are on occupational disease and work injury"); Section 6's negative list gets one new entry (Entry 16).

## Section 13, Entry 13 (Chemical burns)

| URL | Verified | Original quotation |
|---|---|---|
| <https://niohp.chinacdc.cn/kpdw/202507/t20250722_308659.htm> | Yes | Skin contact: "immediately flush the contact area with copious clean water for at least 15 minutes"; eye contact: "immediately flush eyes with copious clean water or saline for at least 15 minutes ... hold the eyelids open to ensure the water flow fully contacts the eyeball and the inside of the eyelids, and do not rub the eyes. Proceed to an ophthalmologist after flushing" |
| <http://yjt.hunan.gov.cn/yjt/tszt/aqzs/201509/t20150919_3113778.html> | Yes | "For poisons absorbed through the skin, or poisons whose corrosiveness causes skin burns, immediately remove contaminated clothing and flush with copious clean water; lukewarm water may also be used; hot water is prohibited"; another passage: "Flush the wound with copious running clean water for 20 to 30 minutes (longer for strong chemicals)" "Do not arbitrarily apply ointment or merbromin to fresh wounds, and do not wrap with dirty cloth" |

Graded B: The two official materials give two different flushing durations, 15 minutes and 20 to 30 minutes, with no unified national standard number. The body states the longer one and the Note notes the discrepancy.

Three deliberate wording choices: (1) "Do not grab another acid or base to neutralize" — the Hunan material writes "copious clean water or other prepared antidote or neutralizing solution," referring to dedicated flushing fluid prepared in advance by the unit, not on-the-spot neutralization; the Note distinguishes this. (2) Dry lime powder and cement powder should be brushed off before flushing — a local National Defense Mobilization Office page further claims on this basis that "concentrated sulfuric acid also must not be flushed with water immediately; first wipe dry with clean cloth strips." This claim is inconsistent across sources and lacks authoritative basis, so the body does not write it; the Note notes this. (3) The legal side connects to the occupational disease catalog's "chemical skin burns" and "chemical eye burns," and Occupational Disease Prevention Law Article 37's treatment cost being borne by the employer.
