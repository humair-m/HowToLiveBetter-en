# issue #44: Section 16, Entry 9 — Add Uric-Acid-Lowering Drug Selection and Urine Alkalinization

2026-09-29. Reader pointed out that Section 16, Entry 9 (long-term uric-acid-lowering medication once gout is diagnosed) only writes allopurinol, and raised four points:

1. Among uric-acid-production inhibitors, the main one is febuxostat, more widely used than allopurinol.
2. Among uricosurics, the main one is benzbromarone, with probenecid and dotinurad also available; dotinurad may become the main one in the future.
3. When taking benzbromarone to excrete uric acid, sodium bicarbonate is recommended to alkalinize the urine.
4. Basis: the Chinese Medical Association's "Chinese Guidelines for the Diagnosis and Treatment of Hyperuricemia and Gout (2019)" and "Grassroots Diagnosis and Treatment Guidelines for Gout and Asymptomatic Hyperuricemia (2024)."

## Handling Decision

The entry's main line is unchanged: confirmed gout requires long-term uric-acid-lowering medication, titrated to a target blood-uric-acid level. What the reader raises is "which drug to choose" — and there is indeed disagreement here; the original entry only wrote the US guideline's first choice without explaining. So the Benefit field was supplemented with pro-and-con evidence at the end, and the Note's opening was given a "disputed" tag.

- Point 1: "More widely used" is current prescribing practice, not evidence. What should be written is the debate over febuxostat's cardiovascular safety — added the CARES and FAST randomized trials.
- Point 2: Added the sentence "Benzbromarone is still available domestically." Probenecid and dotinurad were not written. Only a corporate press release was found for dotinurad's domestic approval — no NMPA original was found; "main drug of the future" is a prediction and not entered into the body text.
- Point 3: This is exactly where there is disagreement, written into the Note. The US guideline conditionally recommends against urine alkalinization, with very-low certainty of evidence, but requires plenty of fluids. The specific recommendation from the domestic guideline could not be retrieved — see below.
- Point 4: The full texts of the domestic guidelines are in paid databases; only abstracts were retrieved. The second document the reader cites is the grassroots diagnosis-and-treatment guideline (2024); not even the abstract was found. The "2024 updated version" mentioned in the body is a different document — the Chinese Medical Association's Endocrinology Branch's "Chinese Guidelines for the Diagnosis and Treatment of Hyperuricemia and Gout (2024)" — these are not the same document.
