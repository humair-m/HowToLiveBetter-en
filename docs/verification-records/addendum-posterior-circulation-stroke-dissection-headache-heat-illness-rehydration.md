## Entry 3 (posterior circulation stroke: vertigo, diplopia, finger-to-nose inaccuracy)

| Literature | Verified | Original numbers |
|---|---|---|
| Aroor S, Singh R, Goldstein LB (2017). BE-FAST (Balance, Eyes, Face, Arm, Speech, Time): Reducing the Proportion of Strokes Missed Using the FAST Mnemonic. Stroke, 48(2), 479-481. <https://doi.org/10.1161/STROKEAHA.116.015169> (PMID 28082668) | Yes (Europe PMC abstract verbatim) | "Of 736 eligible patients with acute ischemic stroke, 14.1% presented without FAST symptoms. When gait imbalance, leg weakness, and visual symptoms were included, the proportion of missed strokes decreased to 4.4%." The authors' conclusion notes "suggested prospective validation before updating public education programs" |

Set B, not A: Single-center (University of Kentucky stroke center) 2014 one-year retrospective analysis, 736 cases with specific numbers but only this one study; the authors themselves call for prospective validation. Benefit magnitude "Large" — metric Mortality; missed diagnosis means missing the thrombolysis window. All three cost components zero, cost-effectiveness tier very high.

The reader's "can't tap the nose with a finger" is the finger-to-nose test, testing cerebellar ataxia, a sign under BE-FAST's Balance arm. The body text writes it as a prompt, not as ruling out stroke: no single bedside maneuver has sufficient negative predictive value, so the writing lands at "if it appears suddenly and doesn't resolve, call 120 (emergency)."

## Entry 5 (aortic dissection)

| Literature | Verified | Original numbers |
|---|---|---|
| Hagan PG, Nienaber CA, Isselbacher EM, et al. (2000). The International Registry of Acute Aortic Dissection (IRAD): new insights into an old disease. JAMA, 283(7), 897-903. <https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.283.7.897> (PMID 10685714) | Yes (Europe PMC abstract verbatim) | 464 cases, 12 centers; "While sudden onset of severe sharp pain was the single most common presenting complaint, the clinical presentation was diverse." In-hospital mortality 27.4%; for type A managed with medication alone (no surgery), mortality 58% |

Set B: Multicenter registry, not a cohort study or trial; 464 cases mid-sized; mortality is an observed value within the registry. Benefit magnitude "Large" — when dissection is mistaken for MI and anticoagulation or thrombolysis is given, it can be fatal; identification's value lies in changing the handling direction. "Pain migrating along the aortic path" and "blood-pressure differential between arms" are textbook signs; the abstract has no proportion that can be cited verbatim, so they are written only as qualitative hints in the Note, without numbers.

Not adopted: The widely circulated "untreated type-A dissection has a 1%–2% per-hour mortality" comes from Hirst's 1958 autopsy series; the verbatim original was not retrieved this round, and is not written into the body text.

## Entry 6 (thunderclap headache and subarachnoid hemorrhage)

| Literature | Verified | Original numbers |
|---|---|---|
| Perry JJ, Stiell IG, Sivilotti MLA, et al. (2013). Clinical decision rules to rule out subarachnoid hemorrhage for acute headache. JAMA, 310(12), 1248-1255. <https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2013.278018> (PMID 24065011) | Yes (Europe PMC abstract verbatim) | 2,131 patients with "headache peaking within one hour" prospective multicenter cohort, 132 (6.2%) subarachnoid hemorrhage; Ottawa SAH Rule sensitivity "100% (95% CI, 97.2%-100.0%)", specificity "15.3% (95% CI, 13.8%-16.9%)"; rule items: age 40 years or older, neck pain or stiffness, witnessed loss of consciousness, onset during exertion, thunderclap headache, limited neck flexion on examination |

Set A: Canadian 10-ED prospective cohort, sample size and sensitivity interval complete. Benefit magnitude "Large"; cost recorded as 钱=少 时间=中 (ER head CT and the half-day waiting for results); cost-effectiveness tier High.

The body text emphasizes "speed of peaking" rather than "pain intensity," because the cohort's inclusion criterion is peaking within one hour; specificity only 15.3% is written into the Note to avoid the reader thinking that meeting one item means high probability of hemorrhage.

## Entry 20 (heat-illness rehydration)

| URL | Verified | Original quotation |
|---|---|---|
| Chinese Center for Disease Control and Prevention, "Public High-Temperature Heatstroke Prevention and Emergency Management Guidelines (2014 edition)" <https://niohp.chinacdc.cn/sndt/201405/t20140530_97623.htm> | Yes | "Regardless of the amount of exercise, fluid intake should be increased; do not wait until thirsty to drink water" "drink at least 2–4 cups of cool water per hour (500–1000 ml); water temperature should not be too high; drink small amounts frequently" "Do not drink alcoholic or high-sugar beverages. These beverages cause greater fluid loss. Also avoid drinking very cold frozen beverages"; heatstroke management explicitly states "do not give the patient water"; cooling methods include "soaking the patient in a tub of cool water; placing the patient under a cool shower; wiping the patient's body with cool water; cool wet towels or ice packs applied to the head, armpits, and groin" |
| US CDC/NIOSH. Heat-Related Illnesses. <https://www.cdc.gov/niosh/heat-stress/about/illnesses.html> | Yes | Heatstroke: "Cool the worker quickly... With a cold water or ice bath, if possible"; heat exhaustion: "Encourage frequent sips of cool water"; heat cramps: "Drink water and have a snack or a drink that replaces carbohydrates and electrolytes (such as sports drinks) every 15 to 20 minutes" "Avoid salt tablets" |

Set B: Both are official handling metrics, no effect size for "how much mortality drops if followed." Benefit magnitude "Medium" — rehydration itself is supportive; what saves lives is cooling and getting to the hospital.

**The reader's statement is both adopted and bounded in the body text**: The China CDC guideline does write "avoid drinking very cold frozen beverages," so "don't gulp ice drinks right after heatstroke" has an official source and is written as-is; but the guideline's reason is fear of stomach cramps, with no quantified evidence, while the same guideline's first-line cooling methods are precisely cold-water immersion, cool shower, and ice packs. So the Note makes clear: cold is for the body, not for the mouth — don't misread "don't drink ice" as "can't use cold." The other half is salt — after heavy sweating, drinking only plain water dilutes blood sodium, and both sources point to electrolyte-containing beverages rather than plain water.
