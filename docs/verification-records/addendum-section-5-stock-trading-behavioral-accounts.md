# Section 5 Addendum: Disposition Effect and Active Trading During Volatile Periods (2026-09-22)

Task origin: GitHub issue #24 (wangchao732). The original text reads "advise everyone not to trade A-shares; 90% of retail investors get trapped. Once trapped, they refuse to cut their losses, and it's nearly impossible to get out within a year. They keep thinking about averaging down as it falls, which harms both life and wealth."

Existing coverage: Section 5 already has a cluster of investment entries — Entry 15 (do not trade stocks frequently, Barber-Odean 2000, the highest turnover group annualized 11.4% vs the market's 17.9%), Entry 16 (do not borrow money or use leverage), Entry 17 (broad-based index funds replacing active funds), Entry 18 (among similar funds, pick the low-fee one), Entry 19 (do not bet on a single underlying), Entry 27 (build an emergency fund first). That is, "do not trade frequently, do not borrow money, do not bet on a single name" were already covered.

What the issue really pointed at that was not covered was two things: ① **bias in sell decisions** (sell when up, hold when down, then keep averaging down), zero coverage in the book; ② **the active trading cost during sharp rise-and-fall periods** — Entry 15 addresses long-term turnover cost and does not cover "trading during a market frenzy," and it cites U.S. data from the 1990s without Chinese figures.

Landing point: Two new entries (Entries 37 and 38) added at the end of Section 5, not inserted into the investment cluster between Entries 15 and 19. The reason is that insertion would have pushed Entries 20–36 down overall, and the seven cross-section references pointing to Section 5 plus a dozen intra-section self-references would all have needed editing; per the CLAUDE.md metric, this kind of sequential shift can also produce diffs in the cross-reference table that are not easy to see. Appending at the end is zero-risk; the search pages are sorted by cost-effectiveness anyway, and readers do not navigate by paper order. Entry 36 (bulk foods) was appended the same way.

## Did not adopt the issue's framing

**Did not write "do not trade A-shares."** Per the two CLAUDE.md rules "only unwind the account, do not give conclusions" and "before judging an entry that only prohibits without offering a way out, first confirm the person really has no choice": Entry 17 already gives a low-cost path for "putting money into the stock market" (broad-based index funds); writing another entry that says "do not buy at all" would directly conflict with it. So these two entries write about differences at the level of action — for the same money, the same market episode, how to set sell rules and how much to trade when the market is hottest — rather than "whether to enter at all."

**Did not write "90% of retail investors lose money."** This figure circulates widely online, but no verifiable official statistics could be found: the statistical yearbooks of the Shanghai and Shenzhen exchanges and the investor condition survey reports do not give a public time series for "share of loss-making accounts," and no citable primary literature was found this round. Per the project rule "rather not write numbers that are uncertain," Entry 38's Note explicitly states that this figure is not used, and gives a verifiable substitute — the loss amount of the smallest 85% of accounts in the same cohort relative to "buy-and-hold."

**"Nearly impossible to get out within a year" was not written.** This is a question of recovery time, which requires computing holding-period returns by entry-point distribution. No citable primary calculation was found this round; it was not written from impression.

## Grading and tier

**Both entries graded A.** Odean 1998 gives ratios, differences, and t values, and the full text is verifiable word for word; An-Lou-Shi uses the exchange's full administrative data and gives absolute amounts and percentages of initial market value. Neither is a meta-analysis, but per the project metric "large cohort + quantifiable numbers" is enough for A.

**Why the Source field includes the working paper link.** The journal's official version is on ScienceDirect, which this machine could not access (prior records noted that publisher sites like tandfonline/Wiley were blocked; this round's test found Wiley showing a Cloudflare challenge and the Springer DOI returning 404). Crossref and Semantic Scholar have no abstract for this paper. So the figures in the entry were verified word for word against the authors' own October 2021 public working paper draft; both links are given in the Source field, and the entry notes that the figures were verified against the latter. The official abstract writes both sides uniformly as "30% of either group's initial equity wealth," while the working paper body writes 28% and 31% separately; the entry uses the latter and also gives "about thirty percent each."

**Entry 37 benefit graded "medium."** Money metric by amount threshold: the verifiable figures are "3.4 percentage points higher over the following year" and the 4.4% from the worked example; on a 100,000-yuan-class account, that is several thousand yuan per year, falling in the "hundreds to thousands" tier. It was not graded "large" because the original text gives no cumulative amount; Entry 15 was graded "large" because that paper gave a long-term annualized gap of 6.5 percentage points.

**Entry 38 benefit graded "large."** The original text gives "equivalent to 28% of this group's initial stock market value"; a 100,000-yuan-class account corresponds to 10,000-yuan-class losses, falling in the "10,000-yuan-class" tier.

**Willpower is graded "some" for both.** Consistent with Entries 15 and 16 in the same cluster: what these two entries ask the reader to change is one decision habit, not a long-term inertia to fight every day.

## Three writing decisions

**Entry 37 openly acknowledges that averaging down is half right.** Saying "averaging down is wrong" directly would be read as an arithmetic error by the reader (buying more does lower the average cost). The Note writes "arithmetically correct, what's wrong is treating it as a way to recover," and points out that its real effect is to increase the position in a single underlying, linking to Entry 19.

**The tax-loss section is marked as the U.S. tax system.** The 4.4% includes the tax-loss benefit of realizing losses early; this part does not hold in China. The Note only writes "China does not transplant this; the rest still holds," without writing the details of the Chinese tax system — that would require separate finance and tax documents and is not the topic of this entry.

**Entry 38's "loss" metric is written into the body.** The 250B in the original text is the active-trading profit/loss relative to buy-and-hold, not a paper loss; the two are very different. The Benefit field adds a sentence: "the 'loss' here is compared against holding still throughout," otherwise the reader would read it as "the market dropped by 250 billion."
