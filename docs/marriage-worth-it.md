# Is Marriage Worth It: Breaking a Confused Account into Five Clear Accounts

What many people want to ask is a sweeping question: is marriage a loss or a gain? That question packs in several things: can the other person provide emotional support, share the housework, earn money, and should you marry to satisfy your elders. These cannot be converted into one another and cannot be answered together, so this piece breaks them apart to calculate. Each account uses only figures from official statistics or meta-analyses (which combine multiple studies); where calculation is not possible, it says so outright. At the end is a fill-in-the-blank checklist you can use yourself; the piece gives no conclusion of "should you marry."

## 1. First break the question apart

At least five accounts are bundled into "is marriage worth it." These five accounts have different units and different methods of calculation; they cannot be added together.

1. Money: One is the actual outlay for bride price, the wedding, the marital home; the other is the legal rules on which property belongs to whom. The first has no official statistics; the second has explicit clauses.
2. Time: Housework, caring for family members, making concessions for the other person's work — these are all unpaid labor; the unit is minutes per day.
3. Health: How much the mortality rate differs between married and unmarried people. This account can only show that the two occur together; it cannot show that the gap is brought about by marriage.
4. Legal risk: How property is divided, how long it takes to exit, which contributions can be claimed back as compensation. This account depends on how the law is written.
5. Relationship quality: Whether the other person gives you emotional support, how badly the two of you fight. This account can only be measured by the strength of association calculated in studies; it cannot be converted into money, nor into time.

The fifth is the hardest to turn into a number, but the closest to what most people actually want to ask.

## 2. Data and sources for each account

### Registration data: the marriage rate and divorce rate are two crude rates

The figures come from the Ministry of Civil Affairs' "2024 Statistical Bulletin on the Development of Civil Affairs." In 2024, 6.106 million couples registered their marriage nationwide, down 20.5% from the previous year, with a marriage rate of 4.3‰. 3.513 million couples had their divorce legally processed, with a divorce rate of 2.5‰. Of these 3.513 million couples, 2.622 million registered their divorce at a civil affairs office, and 891,000 were divorced by court judgment or mediation. The bulletin's note specifies how these two rates are calculated: take the number of couples marrying (or divorcing) in the year, divide by the average total population of that year, and multiply by 1000‰.

The denominators of both rates are the entire population, which is why they are called crude rates. "Crude" means no breakdown was applied. It answers how many couples per thousand people went to register a marriage or a divorce this year; it does not answer whether a given marriage will end. To answer the latter, you need data that follows the same cohort of people over a long period. This piece could not find such data in the official source text.

### Health account: married people have lower mortality, but this is correlation, not causation

Three meta-analyses pooled multiple studies together, and the magnitudes they report are consistent. "All-cause mortality" below means mortality from all causes combined, regardless of the cause.

- Manzoli et al. (2007) pooled 53 comparisons and over 250,000 older adults. "Not in a marriage" here includes three kinds of people: widowed, divorced or separated, and never married. Married people compared with these three had an all-cause mortality RR of 0.88, about 12% lower. RR is the ratio of mortality risk between two groups; less than 1 means the married side is lower. The 95% CI 0.85–0.91 is the confidence interval for this figure. Looking at the three kinds separately: widowed 1.11, divorced or separated 1.16, never married 1.11 — about 10%, 16%, and 10% higher respectively. Splitting by sex, by quality of study, and by Europe vs. the Americas, the results do not change. Recalculated with the most conservative method, RR was 0.94, only about 6% lower.
- Roelfs et al. (2011) pooled 95 papers and over 500 million people. Never-married people compared with married people had an all-cause mortality HR of 1.24, about 24% higher. HR, like RR, is a multiplier; greater than 1 means higher risk. 1.19–1.30 is the confidence interval for this figure. The older the age, the smaller this gap. The more rigorous the study, the smaller the calculated gap.
- Wang et al. (2020) pooled 21 studies and over 7.89 million people. Not being in a marriage was associated with all-cause mortality, cancer mortality, and cardiovascular mortality, in both men and women. The associations were stronger in men for cardiovascular mortality and all-cause mortality. Never-married men compared with never-married women had a 31% higher risk of dying from stroke, and 9% higher all-cause mortality.

All three studies only tracked and recorded populations; they did not have group-based controls. People who are physically healthier, have higher incomes, and have more social ties are more likely to marry in the first place and also more likely to live longer. This is called the selection effect. The Manzoli paper also found signs of publication bias. Publication bias means that studies with favorable results are more likely to be published, so pooling them tends to lean optimistic. So these figures can only be read as "mortality is lower in the married population," not as "if you marry, you will die 12% less." The samples in these studies are dominated by European and American populations; using them domestically can only serve as a rough magnitude reference.

### Time account: the gender gap in unpaid labor is one upper-bound reference

The figures come from the National Bureau of Statistics' Third National Time Use Survey, conducted in May 2024. Three terms first. Unpaid labor means the portion of work for which no one pays. A "participant" means someone who actually did the activity that day. The "participation rate" means the proportion of people who did the activity that day. The results: among participants in unpaid labor, the average time spent per day was 2 hours 45 minutes, of which men 1 hour 52 minutes and women 3 hours 29 minutes. The participation rate was 75.6%, of which men 67.5% and women 83.9%. Looking at housework alone, averaged across all residents it is 1 hour 17 minutes per day. Counting only those who actually did it that day, it is 1 hour 59 minutes, 28 minutes less than in 2018, with a participation rate of 64.9%. Looking at accompanying and caring for family members alone, averaged across all residents it is 30 minutes. Counting only those who actually did it that day, it is 1 hour 46 minutes, with a participation rate of 28.4%.

The 2018 second survey was as follows: housework averaged across all residents was 1 hour 26 minutes, of which men 45 minutes and women 2 hours 6 minutes. The participation rate was 40.4% for men and 75.6% for women. Accompanying and caring for children's daily life, averaged across all residents, was 36 minutes, of which men 17 minutes and women 53 minutes. The participation rate was 12.3% for men and 25.1% for women.

Both surveys were grouped only by sex, urban/rural, and age; they were not grouped by marital status. So the question "how much more housework will you do each day after marrying" has no official figure. What can be used is the gap between men and women. In 2024, the male-female gap in unpaid labor was 1 hour 37 minutes. If the division of housework is not negotiated clearly, this is roughly the maximum gap two people can open up.

### Money account: default rules, written agreements, and compensation for housework

There are no official statistics for bride price, the wedding, and the marital home; this piece gives no figures. What can be written clearly is the rules set by the marriage and family chapter of the Civil Code. The Civil Code took effect on January 1, 2021.

- Article 1062: From the day of marriage to the day of divorce, four kinds of things obtained during this period count as the joint property of both spouses. One: wages, bonuses, labor remuneration. Two: income from doing business and investing. Three: income from intellectual property. Four: inherited property or property given by others. The fourth has an exception: when a will or a gift contract specifies it is given to only one person, it is not joint property. Both spouses have equal rights to dispose of joint property.
- Article 1063: Four kinds of things count as personal property and do not enter joint property at divorce. One: property that existed before marriage. Two: compensation or damages obtained for bodily injury. Three: property that a will or a gift contract specifies is given to only one spouse. Four: personal-use articles of one spouse.
- Article 1065: The two people may agree between themselves on whose property before and during marriage belongs to whom. They may agree that each retains what is theirs, that everything is jointly held, or that part is retained separately and part is jointly held. This agreement must be in writing; once written, it is legally binding on both. If there is no agreement, or the agreement is unclear, the two articles above apply.
- Article 1088: When one spouse has borne more of the burden of raising children, caring for elders, or assisting the other spouse's work, they may claim a sum of compensation from the other spouse at divorce. How much is first negotiated between the two; if they cannot agree, the court decides.

This set of rules applies to both sides. The spouse who earns more should focus on Articles 1062 and 1065. The spouse who does more of the household labor should focus on Article 1088. A property agreement only needs to be in writing; the law does not require it to be notarized.

### Exit cost: the cooling-off period and the conditions for litigation

- Article 1076: When both spouses are willing to divorce, first sign a written divorce agreement, then apply in person at the marriage registration authority. The agreement must state who will raise the children, and how property and debts will be handled. These points must all be the agreed positions of both spouses.
- Article 1077: Within thirty days from the date the registration authority receives the application, if either party regrets it, they may withdraw the application. These thirty days are the commonly called cooling-off period. Within the thirty days after the cooling-off period ends, both spouses must appear in person to apply for the divorce certificate; if they do not, the application is deemed withdrawn.
- Article 1079: When one spouse wants a divorce and the other does not agree, they may first go through mediation, or go directly to court to file. Once in court, the court must also first mediate. When the court confirms that the affection between the spouses has truly broken down and mediation is of no use, it must grant the divorce. In any of the following situations, when mediation fails, the divorce must be granted: bigamy or cohabitation with another, domestic violence or abuse and abandonment, gambling, drug use, or other vicious habits persisting despite repeated correction, or separation due to mutual incompatibility lasting two full years. There is one further situation: when a first judgment denies the divorce, and the two spouses have again separated for one full year, a subsequent suit must result in a divorce.

Of the 3.513 million couples divorced in 2024, 891,000 were divorced via court judgment or mediation. The cooling-off period applies only to administrative divorce; divorce through court action is not subject to the cooling-off period.

### Relationship quality: an account more worth calculating than "married or not"

Robles et al. (2014) pooled 126 studies and over 72,000 people. The conclusion: the higher the marital quality, the better the health. Here, how tightly two things track each other is measured by a number called the effect size r. The closer r is to 0, the more nearly the two things are unrelated. The larger the absolute value of r, the tighter they track. A minus sign in front of r means that when one is high, the other is low. The r for these associations falls between 0.07 and 0.21. Among them, the r for mortality risk was 0.11. For cardiovascular reactivity when the two spouses were in conflict, r = −0.13. These values are not large. But the association between health behaviors such as eating well and health itself is of the same order of magnitude. Some of the results show publication bias (favorable results are more easily published). Some studies specifically tested for sex differences and found essentially none. Most of these studies only tracked and recorded; they had no group controls, so causation cannot be inferred. The Dhindsa et al. (2020) cardiovascular review also notes that satisfaction with marriage and the quality of the marriage have a clear effect on cardiovascular risk.

This piece did not find a meta-analysis that directly compared "a poor-quality marriage" with "not being married" on mortality. So "a bad marriage is worse than not marrying at all" remains an inferred claim with no numbers behind it. What does hold up is: the difference of "married or not" is confounded by selection effects, while "is the marriage good or not" is a separate matter in its own right. Looking only at whether someone is married, without looking at how the marriage is going, leaves the entire account of marital quality unaccounted for.

## 3. How to figure it for yourself: a fill-in-the-blank checklist

Fill in one account at a time. Do not add the figures from different accounts together. For any cell you cannot fill, write "I don't know." "I don't know" is itself useful information.

What the other person can provide (fill in one item at a time)

- Money: how much the other person earns, how much debt they owe, what property they had before marriage. For property during marriage, will the legal default rules apply, or will you write a written agreement. If an agreement is to be written, what goes in it.
- Time: how much unpaid housework and caregiving the other person currently does per day. The national averages can be used as a reference: among those who actually did it that day, men 1 hour 52 minutes and women 3 hours 29 minutes. After marriage, how will these tasks be divided. When children arrive, or when elders need care, how will they be divided then. Between the two of you, whose work can be put on hold.
- Relationship quality: how often the two of you have had conflicts over the past half year, and how each ended. When you were sick, unemployed, or in a low mood, what did the other person do. Conversely, when the other person faced these things, what did you do.
- Emotional value: when you are with the other person, do you feel you have more energy, or have you been drained. This takes a few months together to see; a list of conditions alone will not show it.

What you yourself will pay (fill in one item at a time)

- Money: how much you will pay for the act of marrying itself, including bride price, the wedding, and the marital home. If you do not marry, where this money would go instead.
- Time: estimate how much more housework and caregiving you will do per day. Then estimate how much more time you will spend on social obligations per week.
- Health: whether your sleep, eating, and exercise will get better or worse because of marriage. This is closer to you than the figures from the meta-analyses.
- Exit cost: for an administrative divorce, you first wait through the 30-day cooling-off period, then go to obtain the certificate within the following 30 days. For a court divorce, you must satisfy the situations listed in Article 1079. Whether you can hold up during this period.

How to protect yourself legally (applies to both sides)

- Do you have documentation of pre-marital property, such as a purchase contract, bank statements, a gift contract, or a will. Whether these documents can prove that the property is "pre-existing" or "specified to belong to one party only."
- Should you sign a written property agreement. Whether this agreement covers only pre-marital property, only what is earned during marriage, or both.
- After marriage, which of you does more housework and caregiving. Does the one who does more know that under Article 1088 they can claim a compensation. Are the two of you willing to negotiate the compensation method in advance.
- Debts: for money borrowed in one spouse's name during marriage, which will count as joint debt of both spouses. Does the other spouse know about that money.

Fill in this column separately for "marrying for the sake of the elders"

- What the elders get: peace of mind, face, an end to the urging, someone to look after them in old age.
- Of these, which are truly benefits to the elders, and which are in fact benefits to yourself.
- The costs of the three columns above are all borne by yourself. The benefits the elders get will not automatically turn into your own health, nor into your own relationship quality.
- After writing it out this way, whether the account is heavy or light is for the person filling it out to judge.

## 4. Common mistakes

Confusing the two divorce rates. One is the crude divorce rate, which counts how many couples per thousand people divorced — in 2024 it was 2.5‰. The other is the "divorce-to-marriage ratio," which counts the number of divorces in the year divided by the number of marriages in the year, 351.3 / 610.6 ≈ 57.5%. The numerator and denominator of the divorce-to-marriage ratio are not the same cohort; most of those divorcing this year married many years ago. When the number of marriages falls, this ratio jumps up on its own, so it cannot show that "half of all marriages end in divorce." The Ministry of Civil Affairs' bulletin does not contain this indicator either.

Taking "occur together" to mean "because of." Married people have about 12% lower mortality — this is a result pooled from multiple studies. But the conditions of physical health, higher income, and richer social life simultaneously affect both things: whether a person can marry, and whether a person can live longer. This account can be calculated, but with a discount applied, and it cannot replace a judgment of the specific relationship in front of you.

Treating marriage as a task to complete. Once it becomes a task, you only stare at the status of "married or not," without looking at "how things are going." Yet the effect of relationship quality on health is independent of whether you are married; task-style decisions typically leave this entire account unaccounted for.

Taking national averages as your own numbers. The male-female gap in the time-use survey is a national average. The property rules are the legal defaults that can be changed with a written agreement. National figures can only give you a magnitude and a starting point for negotiation; they cannot give you a conclusion.

Where there are no official figures, do not fill in with folk figures. Bride price, the wedding, and the cost of raising a child have no official statistics, so this piece leaves them blank. Filling in your own actual numbers is more accurate than citing any institution's estimate.
