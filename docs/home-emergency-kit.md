# Home Emergency Kit Checklist: What to Buy, Where to Put It, How Often to Inspect

Corresponds to Entry 26 of Section 1 of the README (stock a fire extinguisher, a fire blanket, an escape breathing mask, and a first-aid kit). This checklist lists what to keep on hand at home; it does not cover how to use them. What to do on the spot for fire escape, CPR, massive bleeding, burns, and carbon monoxide poisoning is all written in Section 13. For how to choose and install smoke alarms and carbon monoxide alarms, see Entry 3 of Section 1. For gas hoses and stoves, see Entry 4 of Section 1.

This section counts the ledger of fewer deaths and fewer injuries, and covers only injuries caused by accidents, not disease. How useful buying the equipment itself actually is — the evidence is not strong; the reason is written in the final section, so read that passage before reading further.

## 1. The official checklist, original text

The Ministry of Emergency Management once issued a "National Basic Version of Suggested Home Emergency Supplies Stockpile List," totaling 11 items. The table below is the original description.

| No. | Item | Original description |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Drinking water | Cover each person's basic drinking water needs for 3 days, at least 3 L/person |
| 2 | Convenience food | Cover each person's basic food needs for 3 days. Convenience food is small in volume and high in calories |
| 3 | Fire extinguisher and fire blanket | For extinguishing incipient fires. The fire blanket can be draped over the body to escape |
| 4 | Respiratory mask | A fire-filter self-rescue respirator, for fire escape use |

Local versions are more detailed. The Beijing Municipal Emergency Management Bureau's checklist for resident households comes in two tiers: a basic version and an expanded version. The basic version is divided into "three categories: emergency items, emergency tools, and emergency medicines." The specific items are: a hand-crank, rechargeable flashlight that can also receive broadcasts; a survival whistle; towels, paper tissues or wet wipes; a respiratory mask; a multi-function combination scissor; an emergency escape rope; a fire extinguisher or fire blanket. Medicines and medical materials are listed separately: anti-infection and anti-cold medicines, medical surgical masks, medical materials such as gauze bandages, iodine cotton swabs. The expanded version is divided into "five categories of emergency supplies: food, personal items, escape and self-rescue tools, medical first-aid supplies, and important documents." This bureau also offered three suggestions. First, "select emergency supplies provided by legally qualified, reputable production and operating enterprises." Second, "prioritize stocking the items in the basic version." Third, "become familiar with the correct use of emergency supplies, inspect the condition of emergency supplies regularly, and promptly replace emergency supplies that have passed their expiration date."

## 2. The three fire-protection items

**Fire extinguisher.** A portable fire extinguisher is a product subject to national compulsory certification. A qualified cylinder is printed with the CCC mark. The standard it implements is GB 4351—2023 "Portable Fire Extinguishers." The kind pre-charged with pressure (except carbon dioxide extinguishers) has a pressure gauge on the cylinder. The needle of the gauge must rest in the green zone. If the needle points into the red or yellow zone, the unit can no longer be used. How many years before scrapping is calculated according to the standard XF 95: water-based 6 years, dry powder 10 years, carbon dioxide 12 years. The age is counted from the date of manufacture. The manufacture date can be found stamped on the cylinder body. For household use, an ABC dry powder extinguisher is typical. Place one by the kitchen doorway, one in the entryway. Do not tuck it into a cabinet directly above the stove. When a fire actually breaks out, that spot is out of reach.

**Fire blanket.** In the official checklist, it is listed under the same item as the extinguisher. It has two uses: cover a flaming oil pan, or drape it over the body to run out. Keep one in a kitchen drawer. It takes no space and does not expire. When the pan catches fire, first turn off the heat, then place the blanket over it. After covering, do not lift it off right away.

**Escape breathing mask.** Its official name is a fire-filter self-rescue respirator. The standard it implements is GB 21976.7—2012 "Building Fire Escape Refuge Equipment — Part 7: Filter-Type Fire Self-Rescue Respirator." This is a current, mandatory national standard. It went into effect on June 1, 2013, and the 2023 review concluded it remains in effect. When buying, look for this standard number, and also for the CCC mark. Buy one for each person in the household. Keep it within arm's reach at the bedside in the bedroom; do not lock it away in a storage room. It uses the toxin-filter canister inside to absorb toxic gases in the smoke; it is single-use, and it only holds up for its nominal rated duration, so once it is on, head outside. What kills most in a fire is smoke. How to escape (crawl low to the ground, feel a door before opening it, take stairs not elevators) is in Section 13, Entry 24. Smoke alarms are in Section 1, Entry 3. These two things should be done before the three items above.

## 3. What to put in a first-aid kit

The official checklist says only one line under this item: "External-use medicine: hemostatic powder, adhesive bandages, gauze bandages, etc., for treating wounds." Section 13 has already verified what on-scene actions to take. Following those actions, putting the following items in a household first-aid kit is reasonable.

- Hemostasis: one windlass tourniquet — the kind with a rod that you twist tight. Plus sterile gauze and bandages, a triangular bandage, medical tape. How to use a tourniquet, when it must not be used, and why "do not loosen to let blood out," see Section 13, Entry 12
- Wound cleaning (getting the wound clean): iodine cotton swabs, normal saline, adhesive bandages, sterile dressings, disposable gloves, scissors, and tweezers
- Burns and scalds: no ointment at all; on scene, do only one thing — run cool tap water over it for 20 minutes, see Section 13, Entry 14 (after a burn, rinse with cool running water for 20 minutes)
- Records: prepare a card. On it, write the family members' allergy history, chronic illnesses, and medicines they take regularly. When emergency personnel arrive, hand the card to them directly

If someone in the household already knows they have a severe allergy, additionally keep an epinephrine auto-injector. It is a prescription medicine; you need a doctor to prescribe it. See Section 13, Entry 15 (how to recognize anaphylactic shock, how to inject).

The evidence grade of this first-aid kit checklist is C. Grade C means it is written from experience and consensus, without direct literature support, because the official document gives only the four characters "external-use medicine" for this item. What specifically to buy is reverse-engineered from the first-aid actions this book has already verified.

## 4. Lighting, communication, and signaling

For a flashlight, follow the official description and buy a waterproof, explosion-proof one. Recharge it or replace its battery once in a while. A survival whistle is for continuously signaling when you are trapped. Blowing a whistle takes less effort than shouting at the top of your lungs, and the sound carries more easily through a noisy environment. A radio is for receiving emergency broadcasts. The Beijing version of the checklist also lists an emergency escape rope and a multi-function combination scissor. The most cost-effective item in this category is a phone power bank — most households already have one.

## 5. Inspect once a year, ten minutes

- Fire extinguisher: check whether the pressure-gauge needle is still in the green zone. Then look at the manufacture date on the cylinder body and calculate whether it has reached the scrapping age
- Smoke alarms and carbon monoxide alarms: press the test button. Replace batteries once a year, see Section 1, Entry 3 (install smoke alarms and carbon monoxide alarms)
- Respiratory mask: check whether the packaging is broken and whether it has passed its nominal expiration date
- First-aid kit: check whether medicines and dressings have expired, whether the tourniquet's buckle still locks securely
- Flashlight and power bank: check whether they still have charge

The Beijing Municipal Emergency Management Bureau's original wording is "inspect the condition of emergency supplies regularly, and promptly replace emergency supplies that have passed their expiration date." You can schedule this on the same day each year that you replace the alarm batteries, and check several items together.

## 6. What you need not buy

- **A household AED (automated external defibrillator).** One costs upward of ten thousand yuan. And there is no evidence supporting a household buying one. What should be done in cardiac arrest is to begin chest compressions immediately, have someone call 120, and at the same time go to the nearest public place to fetch an AED, see Section 13, Entry 1
- **"Fire masks" or "gas masks" without the CCC mark.** Article 24 of the Fire Protection Law provides that fire-protection products within the scope of compulsory product certification "may only be produced, sold, and used after being certified as compliant by an accreditation body with legal qualification, in accordance with the mandatory requirements of national and industry standards." That is, those that have not passed certification may not be sold or used. Also, industrial toxin-filter canisters protect against workshop toxins, which are not the same thing as fire smoke
- **Pre-bundled "emergency kits."** Such kits are mostly padded with cheap flashlights and masks. Buying the extinguisher, fire blanket, mask, and first-aid kit separately is cheaper and makes it easier to verify certifications one by one
- **Stockpiling more than three days of food and water.** The official checklist is calculated at 3 days per person. The extra stockpiled portion mostly ends up thrown out after expiration, see Section 5, Entry 24 (do not stockpile for big promotions)

Source: Standing Committee of the National People's Congress (2021). Fire Protection Law of the People's Republic of China (2021 amendment, Article 24). <https://www.beijing.gov.cn/zhengce/zhengcefagui/qtwj/202307/t20230726_3207767.html>

## 7. How useful these supplies actually are

The evidence in this section is weaker than that of the other entries in Section 1 of this book.

There is a Cochrane systematic review that pooled data from 98 studies and 2,605,044 people. It evaluated whether providing safety education and distributing safety equipment to households was useful. The result was that such practices may somewhat reduce the rate of injuries, IRR 0.89 (95% CI 0.78 to 1.01), about a 10% reduction. But the upper bound of this confidence interval crosses 1 (1 means no difference), so it may also make no difference at all. Among these, the door-to-door in-home efforts did a bit better, IRR 0.75 (95% CI 0.62 to 0.91), about a 25% reduction. Those that only educated without distributing items had IRR 0.78 (95% CI 0.66 to 0.92), about a 20% reduction. What works is more likely the in-home education and in-home modification, not the act of handing out items itself. This review also had one conclusion: there is no evidence that such practices reduced burns, scalds, or poisonings.

In the same review, such practices did make people's safety habits better. More households had working smoke alarms installed, OR 1.81 (95% CI 1.30 to 2.52), about 80% higher. More had pre-planned an escape route, OR 2.01 (1.45 to 2.77), about twice as many. More stored medicines properly, OR 1.53 (1.27 to 1.84), about 50% higher. More had gates at the top of stairs, OR 1.61 (1.19 to 2.17), about 60% higher.

Source: Kendrick D et al. (2012). Home safety education and provision of safety equipment for injury prevention. Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews. <https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD005014.pub3>

So buying everything on the official checklist in one pass does not cost much and counts as reasonable preparation. But do not assume that buying everything makes you safe. What research has repeatedly shown to work is three things: installing smoke alarms, planning in advance how to escape, and moving things in the home that easily hurt people. These three things are either not on the shopping list at all, or occupy only a very small part of it.
