# 4. Don't Waste Time

This section counts only the time accounting. It looks at how many hours per day, week, or year can be saved, or by how much the proportion of tasks done on time can be increased. Time is not converted to lifespan here, nor to money. Where money is involved, only the calculation method is given, not a conclusion. Most entries in this section are supported by a single study or by aggregate statistics from time-use surveys, so the grades are mostly B and C.

### 1. Write "intend to do" as "at what time, where, when X happens do Y"
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=大 口径=时间 -->
- Cost: Free. 1 to 2 minutes per item, writing one "if… then…" sentence. For example, "Tuesday 8 a.m. — sit down at the desk and write the introduction first."
- Plain-language summary: Rewrite "I'm going to do this thing" as "Tuesday 8 a.m. — sit down at the desk and write the introduction first." Pooled across 94 tests, people who wrote it this way had clearly higher goal-achievement rates, a medium-to-large effect. What changes is only how a sentence is written — no money, no effort.
- Benefit: Pooled across 94 independent tests, those who first wrote their intentions as "if… then…" had higher goal-achievement rates. In psychology this writing form is called an implementation intention. Effect size d = 0.65, considered medium-to-large. Three points where it works: starting, holding the line when interrupted, and withdrawing from doomed efforts.
- Evidence grade: A
- Source: Gollwitzer, P. M., & Sheeran, P. (2006). Implementation intentions and goal achievement: A meta-analysis of effects and processes. Advances in Experimental Social Psychology, 38, 69–119. <https://doi.org/10.1016/S0065-2601(06)38002-1>
- Note: This works only for things you actually want to do. If you don't want the goal itself, no number of sentences will help. Also, these studies mostly recruited students and looked at health-related habits; transferring to work scenarios may not work as well.

### 2. Write down exit conditions before starting work
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=些 收益=大 口径=时间 -->
- Cost: Free. Five minutes at start, writing "if by month/day the metric X isn't met, stop." The hard part is following through on that day — stopping in front of others is a bit face-losing.
- Plain-language summary: Before starting, write one sentence: by month/day, if a certain metric isn't reached, stop. Pooled studies show that this kind of writing does help people pull out of doomed efforts. What's saved is all the time you would have continued pouring in.
- Benefit: Pooled implementation-intention research shows that pre-set "if… then…" rules also help people pull out of doomed efforts. The original term is "disengagement from failing courses of action." The time saved is what you would have continued to invest.
- Evidence grade: B
- Source: Gollwitzer, P. M., & Sheeran, P. (2006). Implementation intentions and goal achievement: A meta-analysis of effects and processes. Advances in Experimental Social Psychology, 38, 69–119. <https://doi.org/10.1016/S0065-2601(06)38002-1>
- Note: Exit conditions should be set at the very start, while still calm. Setting them later gets skewed by the costs already sunk. When judging, look only at future investment and future return, see Entry 3. Also, "willingness to pull out" is just one sub-conclusion in the pooled studies — no one has separately calculated how much time it saves.

### 3. When deciding whether to continue, look only at future investment and future return — not at what's already invested
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=些 收益=中 口径=时间 -->
- Cost: Free. The hard part is accepting one fact: the time already invested is gone and can't be recovered.
- Plain-language summary: Money and time already invested make people more likely to keep going. Pooled across 98 datasets, this tendency is stable. So when judging whether to continue, count only what you'd still need to put in and what you'd still get back. Don't count what's already been spent.
- Benefit: Money, energy, and time already invested make people more likely to continue. In psychology this is the sunk-cost effect. A pooled analysis of 98 datasets confirms this tendency is stable. Avoiding it stops you from continuing to pour in time "to avoid waste."
- Evidence grade: B
- Source: Arkes, H. R., & Blumer, C. (1985). The psychology of sunk cost. Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes, 35, 124–140. <https://doi.org/10.1016/0749-5978(85)90049-4>; Roth, S., Robbert, T., & Straus, L. (2015). On the sunk-cost effect in economic decision-making: A meta-analytic review. Business Research, 8(1), 99–138. <https://doi.org/10.1007/s40685-014-0014-8>
- Note: For the Arkes and Blumer theater experiment, we verified only the direction: those who paid higher ticket prices attended more shows over half a year. The specific number of shows attended by each group has not been verified from the original text (to be verified). Roth et al. found that this effect fades over time and that older people are less affected.

### 4. Estimate durations based on the actual time of past similar tasks — not by planning forward
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=大 口径=时间 -->
- Cost: Free. Before estimating, take a few minutes to look up how long the last similar thing actually took.
- Plain-language summary: Students estimated they'd finish a thesis in 33.9 days; actual time was 55.5 days, and only 29.7% finished within their own estimate. Another group was asked to first recall how long the last similar task took and then estimate. Their on-time rate rose from 29.3% to 60.0%.
- Benefit: Students estimated 33.9 days to finish a thesis; actual was 55.5 days. Only 29.7% finished within their own estimate. The researchers asked another group to first connect past experience to the current task before estimating. This group's on-time rate rose from the control's 29.3% to 60.0%. The optimism bias toward underestimating duration no longer appeared in this group.
- Evidence grade: B
- Source: Buehler, R., Griffin, D., & Ross, M. (1994). Exploring the "planning fallacy": Why people underestimate their task completion times. Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 67(3), 366–381. <https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.67.3.366>; Flyvbjerg, B. (2006). From Nobel Prize to project management: Getting risks right. Project Management Journal, 37(3), 5–15. <https://doi.org/10.1177/875697280603700302>; Halkjelsvik, T., & Jørgensen, M. (2012). From origami to software development: A review of studies on judgment-based predictions of performance time. Psychological Bulletin, 138(2), 238–271. <https://doi.org/10.1037/a0025996>
- Note: Buehler's was only Canadian university students, a single study, small sample: 37 people and about 40 per group. Estimating from historical data of similar projects is called reference class forecasting — see Flyvbjerg for engineering specifics. Halkjelsvik's pooled studies note that underestimation is more common than overestimation, but the size of the gap varies a lot by task and method.

### 5. Send an agenda before meetings; don't hold meetings without one; if you can stand, stand
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=中 口径=时间 -->
- Cost: Free. The organizer spends 5 to 10 minutes before the meeting writing an agenda. Standing meetings require participant cooperation.
- Plain-language summary: When an agenda is sent beforehand, participants are more likely to find the meeting useful. In an experiment, sitting meetings were 34% longer than standing ones, and the decisions made were equally good either way.
- Benefit: Two surveys had 958 and 292 people respectively. The result: meetings that sent an agenda beforehand had participants more likely to consider the meeting effective; this relationship was statistically significant. In another experiment, 55 groups sat and 56 groups stood; sitting meetings lasted 34% longer. The quality of decisions did not differ between the two formats.
- Evidence grade: B
- Source: Leach, D. J., Rogelberg, S. G., Warr, P. B., & Burnfield, J. L. (2009). Perceived meeting effectiveness: The role of design characteristics. Journal of Business and Psychology, 24(1), 65–76. <https://doi.org/10.1007/s10869-009-9092-6>; Bluedorn, A. C., Turban, D. B., & Love, M. S. (1999). The effects of stand-up and sit-down meeting formats on meeting outcomes. Journal of Applied Psychology, 84(2). <https://doi.org/10.1037/0021-9010.84.2.277>
- Note: The standing-meeting experiment was 5-person lab groups. Long meetings and large conferences cannot directly apply this conclusion. Leach measured participants' perceived effectiveness, not actual output.

### 6. Cut the number of meetings; if it can be resolved asynchronously in writing, don't meet
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=些 收益=大 口径=时间 -->
- Cost: Free. Needs cooperation from colleagues and superiors. The hard part is that writing a clear paragraph takes more effort than just saying something.
- Plain-language summary: The more meetings in a day, the more tired people are and the more work they feel they have on their hands. Cutting one meeting saves the time of that meeting itself, no discount.
- Benefit: Two surveys of employees working more than 35 hours per week, with 676 and 304 people respectively. How much time meetings take is not a fixed effect on work attitude and well-being; it depends on two things: whether the meeting is well-run, and how much the role depends on others. Another study had people keep a one-week diary; the result was that more meetings in a day meant higher fatigue and higher self-perceived workload. The time saved equals the length of the meeting you cut.
- Evidence grade: B
- Source: Rogelberg, S. G., Leach, D. J., Warr, P. B., & Burnfield, J. L. (2006). "Not another meeting!" Are meeting time demands related to employee well-being? Journal of Applied Psychology, 91(1). <https://doi.org/10.1037/0021-9010.91.1.83>; Luong, A., & Rogelberg, S. G. (2005). Meetings and more meetings: The relationship between meeting load and the daily well-being of employees. Group Dynamics: Theory, Research, and Practice, 9(1). <https://doi.org/10.1037/1089-2699.9.1.58>
- Note: Controversy: Rogelberg 2006 found that the relationship between meeting load and well-being is not one-sided. For jobs that highly depend on others, more meetings isn't necessarily a bad thing. Also, no study has directly compared "switching to asynchronous written communication" with meetings; this entry is the author's inference.

### 7. Break large tasks into subtasks before estimating and starting
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=中 口径=时间 -->
- Cost: Free. The breakdown itself takes 5 to 15 minutes.
- Plain-language summary: When estimating durations, people don't automatically break a task into pieces to count. Listing subtasks first reduces the underestimation. Once broken down, each small step is something you can start on right away, which also reduces procrastination.
- Benefit: When estimating durations, people don't spontaneously break a multi-part task into pieces. For example, writing the literature review, writing the discussion, and formatting the references are several distinct tasks. Listing subtasks first reduces the planning fallacy of underestimating duration. After breaking down, each small step becomes an action that can begin immediately, and procrastination drops in turn. The strongest predictors of procrastination in studies include the aversiveness of the task and the task itself being pushed back.
- Evidence grade: B
- Source: Kruger, J., & Evans, M. (2004). If you don't want to be late, enumerate: Unpacking reduces the planning fallacy. Journal of Experimental Social Psychology, 40(5), 586–598. <https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jesp.2003.11.001>; Steel, P. (2007). The nature of procrastination: A meta-analytic and theoretical review of quintessential self-regulatory failure. Psychological Bulletin, 133(1). <https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.133.1.65>
- Note: How far to break down: aim for "each subtask can be done in one sitting." Over-breaking also wastes time. Also, Kruger and Evans was verified only by direction from the abstract; specific percentages were not verified from the original.

### 8. Set a date yourself for things with no external deadline
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=些 收益=大 口径=时间 -->
- Cost: Free. The hard part is following through — ignoring it on the day is the same as not setting it.
- Plain-language summary: When there's an externally set deadline, 80.6% of students finish before it. Only 38.7% finish within their own estimated time. When something gets done is almost entirely determined by the deadline; self-estimate has little relationship. So for things without a deadline, set one yourself, and honor it.
- Benefit: Students with external deadlines finished before the deadline 80.6% of the time. Only 38.7% finished within their own estimated time. When something gets done follows the deadline closely, correlation r = 0.82 — the closer to 1, the tighter the tracking. The relationship with one's own estimate is only weak.
- Evidence grade: B
- Source: Buehler, R., Griffin, D., & Ross, M. (1994). Exploring the "planning fallacy": Why people underestimate their task completion times. Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 67(3), 366–381. <https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.67.3.366>
- Note: Telling others the date you set makes it closer to an externally set one. What should be noted is that the studies verified the effectiveness of externally set deadlines. Self-set dates — like timeboxing and the Pomodoro technique — have not been directly measured; this entry is inferred.

### 9. Treat procrastination as avoiding discomfort — not as a lack of effort
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=些 收益=中 口径=时间 -->
- Cost: Free. What changes is only the direction of judgment: first ask what about this task is uncomfortable, then find a way.
- Plain-language summary: Procrastination has little relationship with laziness or personality. What best predicts whether someone procrastinates is how unpleasant the task itself is, whether they feel they can do it, and how easily they get pulled away by immediate things. So what helps is making the task less unpleasant and making the first step smaller — not berating yourself and forcing through.
- Benefit: A meta-analysis pooling 691 correlations found that several things have strong and stable associations with procrastination: task aversiveness, time until deadline, self-efficacy (feeling you can do it), impulsivity, and conscientiousness and its components of self-control, distractibility, organization, and achievement motivation. Neuroticism, rebelliousness, and sensation-seeking had only weak associations with procrastination. The authors explained the results with temporal motivation theory: the attractiveness of an activity in the present drops rapidly as the reward moves further away.
- Evidence grade: A
- Source: Steel P (2007). The nature of procrastination: a meta-analytic and theoretical review of quintessential self-regulatory failure. Psychological Bulletin, 133(1), 65–94. <https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.133.1.65>
- Note: Benefit magnitude is "medium" because these studies only point to direction and don't measure how many hours are saved. Concrete actions are in Entry 1 (write "at what time, where, when X happens do Y"), Entry 7 (break large tasks into subtasks), and Entry 8 (set a date for things with no external deadline) of this section. These are pooled correlation studies, so cause and effect can't be established.

### 10. Put things you'll use within reach, and move what you don't want to touch farther away — don't count on resisting on the spot
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=中 口径=时间 -->
- Cost: Free, a few minutes once. Put the phone in another room, leave the book and computer on the desk.
- Plain-language summary: Move something a bit farther, and people take it less. Putting the same snacks farther away clearly reduced how much was eaten — a medium-sized effect. Offering fewer options also clearly reduces how many people pick. These methods don't require on-the-spot willpower; you can set them up in a few minutes.
- Benefit: A Cochrane review including 24 randomized trials. Making food less accessible reduced how much was eaten, standardized mean difference −0.60 (95% CI −0.84 to −0.36, the confidence interval; 12 studies, 1,098 people, low-certainty evidence), a medium effect. Reducing the number of available options reduced selection of the food even more, standardized mean difference −1.13 (95% CI −1.90 to −0.37, 3 studies, 154 people, low-certainty evidence). Regression showed: the farther away and the fewer options, the stronger the effect.
- Evidence grade: B
- Source: Hollands GJ, Carter P, Anwer S, et al. (2019). Altering the availability or proximity of food, alcohol, and tobacco products to change their selection and consumption. Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews, 9, CD012573. <https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD012573.pub3>
- Note: Graded B because: all 24 studies in this review were on food — none on alcohol or tobacco; 14 were in the lab, all from high-income countries. So "move farther, touch less" applied to phones and games is an extrapolation, not directly tested. Putting the phone out of sight was directly tested, see Section 3, Entry 1 (turn off non-essential notifications; while working, put the phone out of sight).

### 11. If you want to make yourself do something, put money on it — but first ask whether you're willing to bet
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=少 时间=少 毅力=些 收益=中 口径=时间 -->
- Cost: Deposit-based schemes require putting up your own money, forfeited if you fail. The trial deposited 150 USD.
- Plain-language summary: To force yourself to follow through, you can either get a bonus when you succeed, or put up a deposit refunded only when you succeed, plus a bonus. Among those willing to participate in both, the deposit group achieved 13 percentage points more. But only 13.7% were willing to put up a deposit, vs 90% willing to take a bonus — so deposits work only for those willing to bet.
- Benefit: A U.S. randomized trial of 2,538 people, randomizing employees and their family or friends to one of four incentive programs or usual care. Reward-based (about 800 USD on success) acceptance 90.0%; deposit-based (put up 150 USD, plus 650 USD reward) 13.7%. Six-month sustained quit rate: the four incentive programs ranged from 9.4% to 16.0%; usual care 6.0%. Reward-based 15.7%, deposit-based 10.2%. But among the 13.7% who were willing to participate in both, the deposit group's six-month quit rate was 13.2 percentage points higher than the reward group (95% CI 3.1 to 22.8, the confidence interval).
- Evidence grade: A
- Source: Halpern SD, French B, Small DS, et al. (2015). Randomized trial of four financial-incentive programs for smoking cessation. New England Journal of Medicine, 372(22), 2108–2117. <https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa1414293>
- Note: This trial tested smoking cessation; transferring to things like writing a thesis or fitness is an extrapolation, so the benefit magnitude is "medium." When choosing a scheme, first check whether you'd actually use it. A method most people won't accept, however strong, is useless to those people. So pick the kind you'll accept; a small bet is still better than none. For how to quit smoking itself, see Section 2, Entry 3 (smoking-cessation drugs).

### 12. Count new habits in months, not in "21 days"
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=是 收益=中 口径=时间 -->
- Cost: Free. What's needed is repeating the same action every day in the same context, for several months.
- Plain-language summary: "21 days to form a habit" has no basis. A 12-week tracking study computed that an action becoming automatic took as little as 18 days for the fast ones and as long as 254 days for the slow ones. Still feeling hard after two or three weeks is normal. Missing one day doesn't reset progress to zero.
- Benefit: 96 volunteers each chose an eating, drinking, or activity action to do daily in a fixed context (e.g., "after breakfast") for 12 weeks, scoring their automaticity each day. 82 had enough data for analysis; the model fit on 62. Days to reach 95% of each person's automaticity ceiling ranged from 18 to 254 days. The study also found that missing one execution opportunity had no substantive effect on the habit-formation process.
- Evidence grade: B
- Source: Lally P, van Jaarsveld CHM, Potts HWW, Wardle J (2010). How are habits formed: Modelling habit formation in the real world. European Journal of Social Psychology, 40(6), 998–1009. <https://doi.org/10.1002/ejsp.674>
- Note: Only one study, 96 people, all doing simple actions (e.g., a piece of fruit after a meal). No one has measured how long complex things take — running three times a week, writing every day. So still feeling hard after two weeks cannot be taken as evidence of failure. To make repetition actually happen, tie the action to a fixed context, see Entry 1 of this section (write "at what time, where, when X happens do Y").

### 13. If procrastination is already affecting your life, use self-help materials backed by randomized trials — no need to find a therapist
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=中 毅力=些 收益=中 口径=时间 -->
- Cost: A self-help book is tens of yuan, some are free. Invest about ten weeks, reading one chapter and doing one exercise per week.
- Plain-language summary: Procrastination severe enough to cause suffering is treatable. Following cognitive-behavioral self-help materials for ten weeks — guided or fully self-directed — both clearly outperform doing nothing. Guided and unguided showed no difference, so doing it yourself works.
- Benefit: 150 people randomized into three groups: therapist-guided self-help, unguided self-help, and waitlist control. After ten weeks, the between-group effect size on the pure procrastination scale was d = 0.70 (95% CI 0.29–1.10, the confidence interval) for guided, d = 0.50 (0.10–0.90) for unguided; on the irrational procrastination scale, 0.81 (0.40–1.22) and 0.69 (0.29–1.09) respectively. The proportion reaching clinically meaningful improvement: 31.3% to 40.0% for guided, 24.0% to 36.0% for unguided. The two groups showed no significant difference on any outcome.
- Evidence grade: A
- Source: Rozental A, Forsell E, Svensson A, Andersson G, Carlbring P (2015). Internet-based cognitive-behavior therapy for procrastination: A randomized controlled trial. Journal of Consulting and Clinical Psychology, 83(4), 808–824. <https://doi.org/10.1037/ccp0000023>
- Note: Subjects were Swedish adults using Swedish-language materials; no equivalent Chinese set exists, so benefit magnitude is "medium." The components are the usual few: behavioral activation, behavioral experiments, stimulus control, and psychoeducation on motivation and work methods. Stimulus control is in Entry 10 of this section (move what you don't want to touch farther away). If procrastination is accompanied by clear low mood or anxiety, first follow Section 3, Entry 19 (move, get sun, sleep on time, talk to someone, call 12356).

### 14. Use your own hourly wage to decide which household chores to outsource
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=多 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=大 口径=时间 -->
- Cost: Outsourcing costs money; how much depends on the service. Also need to spend time once to figure out your hourly wage.
- Plain-language summary: Chinese people spend an average of 1 hour 17 minutes per day on housework. Among those who actually did housework on a given day, 1 hour 59 minutes. Those who spend money to buy time have higher life satisfaction. The same amount of money spent on buying time brings more momentary happiness than buying things.
- Benefit: Chinese residents spend an average of 1 hour 17 minutes per day on housework. Among those who did housework, 1 hour 59 minutes on average. This is from a 2024 survey covering 38,500 households and 107,000 people. Another study sampled 6,271 people across four countries; those who spent money to buy time had higher life satisfaction. The researchers also ran an experiment in real life: the same amount of money spent on buying time brought higher momentary happiness than buying physical goods.
- Evidence grade: B
- Source: Whillans, A. V., Dunn, E. W., Smeets, P., Bekkers, R., & Norton, M. I. (2017). Buying time promotes happiness. Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences. <https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1706541114>; National Bureau of Statistics (2024). Communiqué on the Third National Time Use Survey (No. 2). <https://www.stats.gov.cn/sj/zxfb/202410/t20241031_1957216.html>
- Note: First calculate hourly wage: monthly after-tax income ÷ monthly actual working hours. Then look at how much the chore costs to outsource. If the outsourcing price is below your hourly wage, and the time saved will actually be used for earning or resting, outsource. Everyone's wage and outsourcing prices differ; the conclusion is one to calculate yourself. Also, Whillans measured whether people are happier after spending money to buy time, not how many hours are saved.

### 15. Set hard caps on short videos and aimless scrolling
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=是 收益=大 口径=时间 -->
- Cost: Free. The action takes minutes: turn off push, delete the app, or set a daily time limit on the app. The hard part is controlling your hands — resisting the urge to open it.
- Plain-language summary: Chinese people spend an average of 5 hours 37 minutes per day online. Among those who actually went online on a given day, 6 hours 3 minutes. In 2018, by the same calculation, it was only 2 hours 42 minutes. This is the entry with the largest numbers in this section. Cutting one hour a day gains 365 hours in a year.
- Benefit: Chinese residents spend an average of 5 hours 37 minutes per day online. Among those who actually used the internet on a given day, 6 hours 3 minutes. The proportion who used the internet on a given day was 92.9%. These are 2024 figures. In 2018, by the same calculation, it was 2 hours 42 minutes. This is the entry with the largest numbers in this section. Cutting one hour a day equals 365 more hours per year. According to the China Internet Network Information Center, as of June 2025, netizens spent an average of 30.6 hours per week online, 1.9 hours more than half a year earlier. Short-video users numbered 1.068 billion, 95.1% of netizens.
- Evidence grade: C
- Source: National Bureau of Statistics (2024). Communiqué on the Third National Time Use Survey (No. 2). <https://www.stats.gov.cn/sj/zxfb/202410/t20241031_1957216.html>; National Bureau of Statistics (2019). Communiqué on the 2018 National Time Use Survey. <https://www.stats.gov.cn/sj/zxfb/202302/t20230203_1900224.html>; China Internet Network Information Center (2025). 56th Statistical Report on China's Internet Development. <https://www.cnnic.net.cn/NMediaFile/2025/0730/MAIN1753846666507QEK67ZS9DH.pdf>
- Note: Online time includes work, study, and social — not all waste. Graded C because no study has calculated "how much is saved after limiting and where the time goes"; we have only aggregate statistics. For the "looking at how others are doing" kind, a randomized trial measured how much is saved and how mood changes, see Section 3, Entry 21.

### 16. Don't watch TV or scrolling news; for information you need, check it on a fixed schedule in batches
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=些 收益=大 口径=时间 -->
- Cost: Free. The hard part is breaking the habit of opening it casually, and possibly missing some instant messages.
- Plain-language summary: Americans 15 and over watch 2.6 hours of TV per day, taking up half of all their leisure time. Chinese people watch an average of 1 hour 40 minutes per day; those 75 to 84 watch 3 hours 16 minutes. Changing what you need to watch into scheduled batch viewing saves that whole chunk.
- Benefit: Americans 15 and over watch 2.6 hours of TV per day. Their total leisure time is 5.2 hours, so TV takes half. These are 2025 figures. Chinese residents watch an average of 1 hour 40 minutes per day; those aged 75 to 84 watch 3 hours 16 minutes. These are 2018 figures.
- Evidence grade: C
- Source: U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics (2026). American Time Use Survey — 2025 Results. <https://www.bls.gov/news.release/atus.nr0.htm>; National Bureau of Statistics (2019). Communiqué on the 2018 National Time Use Survey. <https://www.stats.gov.cn/sj/zxfb/202302/t20230203_1900224.html>
- Note: We have only aggregate statistics. No study has calculated where the saved time goes after watching less TV and news. "Batch viewing" is the author's experience.

### 17. Spend time once to learn keyboard shortcuts and automation for the software you use often
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=中 毅力=些 收益=中 口径=时间 -->
- Cost: Free. One-time investment of several hours to tens of hours. The hard part is that, at first, it's slower than using the mouse.
- Plain-language summary: Most people who have used Word for years still click toolbar icons. In grouped-comparison experiments, shortcuts were faster than menus and icons. Learning this once is a one-time cost; afterward, every operation every day pays it back. The longer you do the same kind of work, the more you earn back.
- Benefit: A study looked at 251 experienced Word users; most rarely used the faster shortcuts and still mainly clicked icon toolbars. Grouped-comparison experiments confirmed that of menus, icons, and shortcuts, shortcuts were fastest. Saved time accumulates with each daily operation. People doing the same kind of work long-term benefit most.
- Evidence grade: B
- Source: Lane, D. M., Napier, H. A., Peres, S. C., & Sándor, A. (2005). Hidden costs of graphical user interfaces: Failure to make the transition from menus and icon toolbars to keyboard shortcuts. International Journal of Human-Computer Interaction. <https://doi.org/10.1207/s15327590ijhc1802_1>
- Note: Learn only the operations you use more than ten times a day; learning too comprehensively is itself a waste. Also, that efficiency experiment had only 6 participants, and the abstract did not give seconds saved per operation.

### 18. When choosing where to live, put commute time first — shorten one-way commute
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=多 时间=中 毅力=否 收益=大 口径=时间 -->
- Cost: High. May require paying higher rent or getting a smaller place. Moving itself also takes time.
- Plain-language summary: The longer the commute, the worse people feel about their lives. This loss is not made up in wages or housing. Cutting 30 minutes off the one-way commute saves 5 hours per week.
- Benefit: Chinese residents spend an average of 50 minutes per day on transportation. Among those who actually traveled on a given day, 1 hour 2 minutes. These are 2024 figures. German panel data tracking the same people over time show that longer commute is consistently associated with lower subjective well-being (how people feel about their own lives). And this loss is not compensated by income or housing. A review synthesizing multiple studies also confirms that the longer the commute, the less satisfied people are with the commute — independent of transportation mode. Cutting 30 minutes off one-way saves about 5 hours per week.
- Evidence grade: B
- Source: Stutzer, A., & Frey, B. S. (2008). Stress that doesn't pay: The commuting paradox. Scandinavian Journal of Economics, 110(2), 339–366. <https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9442.2008.00542.x>; Chatterjee, K., et al. (2020). Commuting and wellbeing: A critical overview of the literature with implications for policy and future research. Transport Reviews, 40(1), 5–34. <https://doi.org/10.1080/01441647.2019.1649317>; National Bureau of Statistics (2024). Communiqué on the Third National Time Use Survey (No. 2). <https://www.stats.gov.cn/sj/zxfb/202410/t20241031_1957216.html>
- Note: Controversy: Chatterjee et al., after pooling studies, note that no stable relationship is yet visible between commute length and overall life satisfaction. They argue that people generally can use a better job or housing to compensate for the loss from a long commute. Also note: transportation activity statistics count all travel, not just commutes. Also, if you can shift your hours off peak, the same stretch of road can be done faster.
