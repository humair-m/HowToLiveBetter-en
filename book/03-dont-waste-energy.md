# 3. Don't Waste Energy

This section counts only the energy and time accounting. It looks at four things: the attention you can use each day, how fast you react, how many errors you make, and how long it takes to come back after being interrupted. All numbers are directly measured, from experiments or from records following a group of people (cohort studies). These numbers are not converted into lifespan and not into mortality. Most entries in this section are graded B. This is just how the field is — it does not mean the recommendations don't hold. Entry 20 is about expectations when dealing with police, doctors, and bank clerks; it cites regulations, not experiments, so it is graded C. Entry 15 (treat pessimistic thoughts as a symptom) and Entry 23 (treat "others demand I be perfect" as a symptom) cite mortality and suicide-risk numbers, with a different metric than the rest of this section, so they are not compared alongside the others.

### 1. Turn off non-essential notifications; while working, put the phone out of sight
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=中 口径=时间 -->
- Cost: Free. A few minutes to change settings, almost no willpower needed.
- Plain-language summary: A phone notification sound, even if you don't look or touch, drops attention-task performance. The drop is comparable to actually taking a call or sending a text. There's another case: with the phone sitting on the desk, even if you resist looking, the spare capacity your brain can use shrinks. The more phone-dependent you are, the bigger the drop.
- Benefit: Just receiving a notification, without looking or touching, makes attention-task performance noticeably worse. The drop is comparable to actually taking a call or sending a text. Done in a lab, with university-student subjects. Two other experiments showed: even when successfully resisting the urge to look, having one's own phone nearby reduces the spare cognitive capacity the brain can use. Those with stronger phone dependence dropped most.
- Evidence grade: B
- Source: Stothart, Mitchum & Yehnert (2015). The attentional cost of receiving a cell phone notification. Journal of Experimental Psychology: Human Perception and Performance. <https://doi.org/10.1037/xhp0000100>; Ward, Duke, Gneezy & Bos (2017). Brain Drain: The Mere Presence of One's Own Smartphone Reduces Available Cognitive Capacity. Journal of the Association for Consumer Research. <https://doi.org/10.1086/691462>
- Note: These are single lab tasks. What accumulates day after day at this rate has not been measured. For roles that genuinely need to be on call, you can keep notifications on for only a small set of contacts.

### 2. Fix your wake time, even on weekends
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=些 收益=中 口径=时间 -->
- Cost: Free. Sleeping in less on weekends takes a bit of willpower.
- Plain-language summary: The one-fifth with the most irregular schedules have biological clocks about 2.5 hours later than the most regular one-fifth — equivalent to living in chronic jet lag. The more regular the schedule, the better the academic performance.
- Benefit: 61 university students recorded 30 days. The one-fifth with the most irregular schedules had a biological clock (circadian rhythm) about 2.5 hours later than the most regular one-fifth. Specifically, the melatonin-onset time was 00:08 vs 21:32. The most-fatigued, most-want-to-sleep time was also about 1.8 hours later. The more regular the schedule, the better the academic performance (correlation r = 0.37).
- Evidence grade: B
- Source: Phillips et al. (2017). Irregular sleep/wake patterns are associated with poorer academic performance and delayed circadian and sleep/wake timing. Scientific Reports. <https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-017-03171-4>
- Note: Only one study, few people, no grouping control. The researchers inferred that the biological-clock difference comes mainly from differences in daily light exposure. So fixing wake time needs to be paired with morning light exposure to work. For how to recover after an occasional late night, see Section 2, Entry 39 (catch up the next night after staying up late): the method is to go to bed early that night, not sleep until noon the next day.

### 3. Sleep 7 to 8 hours a night — don't treat 6 hours as enough
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=多 毅力=些 收益=大 口径=时间 -->
- Cost: Free. Set aside 1 to 2 more hours per day for sleep. Going to bed earlier takes some willpower.
- Plain-language summary: Sleeping only 6 hours a night for two weeks drops brain performance to the level of two full nights without sleep. The trouble is you can't feel it yourself. Self-rated sleepiness rises in the first few days and then stops rising — those sleeping 6 hours rate themselves as sleepy as those sleeping 4. So "I'm fine on 6 hours" is itself a symptom of sleep deprivation.
- Benefit: 48 healthy adults aged 21 to 38 slept only 6 hours per night for 14 consecutive days. Their cognitive performance declined cumulatively, dropping to the level of two full nights without sleep. Self-rated sleepiness rose only in the first few days, then barely increased. The 6-hour and 4-hour groups did not differ in self-rated sleepiness. This was a randomized controlled trial (RCT). Separately, brief total sleep deprivation (acute sleep deprivation, under 48 hours) had effect size g = -0.776 on simple-attention lapses and g = -0.125 on reasoning accuracy. This part pooled 70 studies.
- Evidence grade: A
- Source: Van Dongen, Maislin, Mullington & Dinges (2003). The cumulative cost of additional wakefulness. Sleep. <https://doi.org/10.1093/sleep/26.2.117>; Lim & Dinges (2010). A meta-analysis of the impact of short-term sleep deprivation on cognitive variables. Psychological Bulletin. <https://doi.org/10.1037/a0018883>
- Note: "Not feeling sleepy" is not evidence of having slept enough. Long-term 6-hour sleepers just feel accustomed; performance does not keep up. The pooled-studies portion refers to total night deprivation. "Chronic short sleep" is supported by only one randomized controlled trial.

### 4. Don't touch caffeine after 2 p.m.
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=些 收益=大 口径=时间 -->
- Cost: Free. Those used to afternoon coffee need willpower to hold off.
- Plain-language summary: A large coffee (400 mg) 6 hours before bed costs more than an hour of sleep that night, as measured by instruments. Yet the sleep diary you write yourself won't show any difference. A regular coffee (107 mg) needs to be finished more than 8.8 hours before bed to not affect sleep. For someone sleeping at 11 p.m., 2 p.m. is the cutoff.
- Benefit: 400 mg of caffeine 6 hours before bed reduced total sleep time measured by instruments by 1.1 to 1.2 hours vs placebo. Placebo is a caffeine-free control drink. Yet subjects' own sleep diaries reported no difference — they couldn't detect it. A pooled analysis (meta-analysis): caffeine on average reduces total sleep time by 45 minutes. The proportion of time in bed actually spent asleep (sleep efficiency) drops 7%. A regular coffee (107 mg) needs to be finished more than 8.8 hours before bed to not affect sleep.
- Evidence grade: A
- Source: Drake, Roehrs, Shambroom & Roth (2013). Caffeine effects on sleep taken 0, 3, or 6 hours before going to bed. Journal of Clinical Sleep Medicine. <https://doi.org/10.5664/jcsm.3170>; Gardiner et al. (2023). The effect of caffeine on subsequent sleep: A systematic review and meta-analysis. Sleep Medicine Reviews. <https://doi.org/10.1016/j.smrv.2023.101764>
- Note: Drake's was done in only one lab, and 400 mg is on the high side — about two or three coffees. Slow metabolizers of caffeine need an even earlier cutoff. What less caffeine buys back is sleep duration, which stacks with Entry 3 (sleep 7 to 8 hours a night).

### 5. Batch-process email and messages into a few fixed times per day
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=些 收益=中 口径=时间 -->
- Cost: Free. Takes some time to tell colleagues how often you reply. Resisting the urge to check email constantly takes some willpower.
- Plain-language summary: The same people were compared over two weeks. The week when email was limited to 3 checks per day had clearly lower daily stress, and the number of emails sent and received was the same. Without restrictions, people checked email 15.5 times per day on average.
- Benefit: 124 adults, the same people comparing two weeks (self-control). The week with email limited to 3 daily checks had clearly lower daily stress than the unrestricted week (effect size Cohen's d = 0.37). The number of emails received and replied to over two weeks did not differ. Without restrictions at the start, people checked email 15.5 times per day on average.
- Evidence grade: B
- Source: Kushlev & Dunn (2015). Checking email less frequently reduces stress. Computers in Human Behavior. <https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chb.2014.11.005>
- Note: Only one study, moderate-to-small effect, measures stress not output. Instant messaging has no direct study; this is inferred from the email case.

### 6. When doing work that needs continuous thought, block out even a few seconds of interruption
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=大 口径=时间 -->
- Cost: Free. Close the door, put on headphones, or hang a "do not disturb" sign.
- Plain-language summary: An average 2.8-second interruption doubles the error rate on the next operation. A 4.4-second interruption triples it. Field tracking also found that the interrupted task takes an average of 25 minutes to return to, with two other things inserted in between. Interrupted people work faster, but stress, frustration, and effort all rise.
- Benefit: An average 2.8-second interruption doubles the step-error rate on sequential tasks. A 4.4-second interruption triples it. Done in a lab. Another study was field observation of 24 office knowledge workers. They switched work topics every 11 minutes on average. 57% of work topics were interrupted. Returning to the interrupted task on the same day took an average of 25 minutes 26 seconds. 2.26 other things were inserted in between. Another experiment with 48 people: interrupted people did things faster, but reported higher stress, frustration, time pressure, and effort.
- Evidence grade: B
- Source: Altmann, Trafton & Hambrick (2014). Momentary interruptions can derail the train of thought. Journal of Experimental Psychology: General. <https://doi.org/10.1037/a0030986>; Mark, Gonzalez & Harris (2005). No task left behind? Examining the nature of fragmented work. CHI 2005. <https://doi.org/10.1145/1054972.1055017>; Mark, Gudith & Klocke (2008). The cost of interrupted work: More speed and stress. CHI 2008. <https://doi.org/10.1145/1357054.1357072>
- Note: The field study split interruptions into two types: people coming to find you, and things you click open yourself. Closing the door only blocks the first type. The second needs to be paired with Entry 1 (turn off non-essential notifications) and Entry 5 (batch-process messages).

### 7. Do one thing at a time — don't reply to messages during meetings
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=些 收益=中 口径=时间 -->
- Cost: Free. Holding back from replying during meetings takes some willpower.
- Plain-language summary: Task switching makes reactions slower and more error-prone. People who routinely have several screens open at once have not trained an ability to do several things at once; they perform worse on task-switching tests.
- Benefit: After task switching, reactions are clearly slower and more error-prone. This part comes from a review of multiple studies. Another lab study used university-student subjects. People who routinely use several kinds of media at once (heavy media multitaskers) are more easily pulled off task by irrelevant stimuli and irrelevant memories. They performed worse on task-switching tests.
- Evidence grade: B
- Source: Monsell (2003). Task switching. Trends in Cognitive Sciences. <https://doi.org/10.1016/S1364-6613(03)00028-7>; Ophir, Nass & Wagner (2009). Cognitive control in media multitaskers. PNAS. <https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.0903620106>
- Note: How much extra switching costs varies a lot with the work; no single number given here. Ophir's was a same-time-point comparison, so it can't distinguish whether "multitasking makes attention worse" or "people with worse attention prefer multitasking."

### 8. No light-emitting screens in the hour before bed — if you must read, use paper or an e-ink screen
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=是 收益=中 口径=时间 -->
- Cost: Free. Putting down the phone an hour before bed requires long-term resistance to habit and willpower.
- Plain-language summary: Reading a light-emitting screen before bed makes you fall asleep about 10 minutes later than reading a paper book. Melatonin is suppressed by 55%. The next day the biological clock is pushed back more than 1.5 hours — sleepier in the morning, takes longer to fully wake.
- Benefit: In a lab, the same people did both conditions in turn (crossover trial). Reading a light-emitting e-book before bed delayed sleep onset by about 10 minutes vs paper book (25.7 minutes vs 15.8 minutes). Evening melatonin was suppressed by 55%. The next day's biological clock (circadian rhythm) was delayed by more than 1.5 hours — melatonin onset at 22:31 vs 21:01. The next morning they were sleepier and took longer to fully wake.
- Evidence grade: B
- Source: Chang, Aeschbach, Duffy & Czeisler (2015). Evening use of light-emitting eReaders negatively affects sleep, circadian timing, and next-morning alertness. PNAS. <https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1418490112>
- Note: Few people, lab-controlled conditions. At everyday phone brightness and usage duration, the effect may be smaller. The study did not test how much the phone's night mode helps.

### 9. Sleep on time — don't stay up for games, short videos, or porn
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=是 收益=大 口径=时间 -->
- Cost: Free. The hard part is resisting the "one more episode" inertia long term. Keeping the phone out of the bedroom is the easiest way.
- Plain-language summary: In 2025, Chinese people slept an average of 6.97 hours per night, falling asleep at 0:10 on average. Those who used phones or tablets before bed had more than double the probability of insufficient sleep and 2.7 times the probability of daytime sleepiness. Even just having the device in the bedroom unused gave similar numbers. So the easiest fix is to keep the phone out of the bedroom.
- Benefit: In 2025, Chinese residents slept an average of 6.97 hours per night, with average sleep-onset time of 0:10 (Chinese Sleep Research Society). Those who used portable devices before bed had about 2.17 times the likelihood of insufficient sleep (OR 2.17). Poor sleep quality about 1.46 times (OR 1.46). Daytime sleepiness about 2.72 times (OR 2.72). Even just having the device in the bedroom without using it gave similar numbers, about 1.79, 1.53, and 2.27 times (OR 1.79/1.53/2.27). This part pooled 20 cross-sectional surveys and 125,198 children and adolescents. Another cross-sectional survey: among 844 adults aged 18 to 94, those who used their phone after lights-out fell asleep more slowly, had lower sleep efficiency, and were more fatigued in the daytime.
- Evidence grade: B
- Source: Carter et al. (2016). Association Between Portable Screen-Based Media Device Access or Use and Sleep Outcomes. JAMA Pediatrics. <https://doi.org/10.1001/jamapediatrics.2016.2341>; Exelmans & Van den Bulck (2016). Bedtime mobile phone use and sleep in adults. Social Science & Medicine. <https://doi.org/10.1016/j.socscimed.2015.11.037>; Chinese Sleep Research Society (2026). 2026 China Sleep Health Research White Paper (Health News report reposted by Fujian Provincial Health Commission). <https://wjw.fujian.gov.cn/jggk/csxx/xcc/mtbd/202603/t20260323_7114382.htm>; Grubbs JB et al. (2018). Pornography Problems Due to Moral Incongruence: An Integrative Model with a Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis. Archives of Sexual Behavior. <https://doi.org/10.1007/s10508-018-1248-x> (one of the controversy sides in the Note); Prause N, Pfaus J (2015). Viewing Sexual Stimuli Associated with Greater Sexual Responsiveness, Not Erectile Dysfunction. Sexual Medicine. <https://doi.org/10.1002/sm2.58> (the other controversy side in the Note)
- Note: Controversy. The cost of staying up itself is in Entry 3 of this section (sleep 7 to 8 hours a night). What effect pornography has on the energy and mind of ordinary people — currently no reliable evidence says clearly whether it's the cause. A pooled analysis concluded that so-called "pornography problems" come more from a mismatch between one's beliefs and one's own behavior than from the content itself. Another study pooled data from 280 men. The result was that viewing duration had no relationship with erectile function during sex with a partner. This entry targets only its displacement of sleep time. ### 10. Don't make major decisions or send important messages late at night
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=些 收益=小 口径=时间 -->
- Cost: Free. Just postpone the matter until after you've slept.
- Plain-language summary: When well-rested, people quickly learn to avoid high-risk options. After two nights without sleep, they pick high-risk options over and over, and older people show this more. Exactly how much a few hours of lost sleep affects things has not been measured, but important decisions should still wait until after sleep.
- Benefit: 34 healthy people performed the Iowa Gambling Task after 49.5 hours without sleep. This is an experiment measuring risk decision-making. When well-rested, they could quickly learn to avoid high-risk options. After sleep deprivation, they chose high-risk options more. Older people were more affected. This is one experiment, only this one study.
- Evidence grade: B
- Source: Killgore, Balkin & Wesensten (2006). Impaired decision making following 49 h of sleep deprivation. Journal of Sleep Research. <https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1365-2869.2006.00487.x>
- Note: The study looked at the extreme case of two consecutive nights without sleep. How ordinary people staying up until 2 a.m. are affected has not been directly measured. The "don't send messages" half is the author's experience (C grade); sending the next day is fine.

### 11. When sleepy in the afternoon, nap 10 minutes — not half an hour
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=中 口径=时间 -->
- Cost: Free. 10 minutes, plus finding a place to lie down or rest your head.
- Plain-language summary: A 10-minute nap refreshes you on the spot, and the effect lasts two and a half hours. 5 minutes does basically nothing. 20 minutes takes half an hour to kick in. After 30 minutes, you wake up groggy first, and during that groggy stretch you perform worse than if you hadn't napped at all.
- Benefit: After a night of insufficient sleep, a 10-minute nap immediately improved sleepiness, fatigue, energy, and cognitive performance, with some benefit persisting past 155 minutes. 5 minutes gave almost no benefit. 20 minutes' benefit appeared only after 35 minutes. After 30 minutes there was first a stretch of sleep inertia: right after waking, performance was worse, then improved.
- Evidence grade: B
- Source: Brooks & Lack (2006). A brief afternoon nap following nocturnal sleep restriction: Which nap duration is most recuperative? Sleep. <https://doi.org/10.1093/sleep/29.6.831>
- Note: Only one lab study, all subjects healthy young people. If you can't sleep, don't force it. No evidence supports whether lying still with eyes closed for 10 minutes helps. Napping more than an hour is associated with higher mortality and coronary heart disease risk, see Section 2, Entry 38 (keep naps under half an hour).

### 12. In open-plan offices, use earplugs or find a quiet room for memory work
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=少 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=小 口径=时间 -->
- Cost: Earplugs are tens of yuan. Booking a quiet meeting room takes some time.
- Plain-language summary: When background noise rises from 39 dB to 51 dB, you remember fewer words, feel more tired, and are less willing to work. During breaks, watching a nature film with sound restores energy better than continuing to listen to office noise.
- Benefit: Students worked in a simulated open-plan office. When background noise rose from 39 dB to 51 dB (dB, LAeq), they remembered fewer words, rated themselves more fatigued, and less willing to work. Watching a nature film with sound during breaks restored the energy score better than listening only to a river sound or to office noise.
- Evidence grade: B
- Source: Jahncke, Hygge, Halin, Green & Dimberg (2011). Open-plan office noise: Cognitive performance and restoration. Journal of Environmental Psychology. <https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jenvp.2011.07.002>
- Note: Only one lab study. The most disruptive part of the noise is speech whose content can be understood. White-noise headphones help with this, but this study did not test headphones.

### 13. Don't work more than 49 hours per week
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=少 时间=少 毅力=些 收益=大 口径=时间 -->
- Cost: Possibly less overtime pay. May take effort to negotiate with your supervisor.
- Plain-language summary: Records from British munitions factories in World War I show that within 49 hours per week, output was proportional to hours. Beyond 49 hours, each extra hour yielded less and less. Working 70 hours a week produced almost the same output as 56 hours — the extra 14 hours were wasted.
- Benefit: Output records of workers in British munitions factories in World War I: below 49 hours per week, output was proportional to hours. Above 49 hours, the growth rate of output began to slow. Output peaked at around 63 hours. Working 70 hours produced almost no more than 56 — the extra 14 hours were wasted.
- Evidence grade: B
- Source: Pencavel (2015). The Productivity of Working Hours. The Economic Journal. <https://doi.org/10.1111/ecoj.12166>
- Note: The data come from factories a hundred years ago, doing physical labor, with mostly female workers. This threshold may not be the same for mental work; the author also says other occupations may be higher or lower. What's reliable is the direction: past a certain point, more hours yield almost no more output. The specific number 49 is not reliable.

### 14. When you catch yourself ruminating on the same bad thing, switch to something that uses your hands
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=些 收益=中 口径=时间 -->
- Cost: Free. The hard part is first noticing you're ruminating (called "rumination") — noticing itself takes practice.
- Plain-language summary: Repeatedly thinking about the same bad thing doesn't produce an answer. It only deepens depression, anchors negative thoughts more firmly, makes you less able to solve problems and less likely to act, and exhausts the people around you who are willing to listen. When you notice you're going in circles, go do something with your hands.
- Benefit: A review of multiple studies says ruminating on the same bad thing (rumination) worsens depression. It also reinforces negative thinking and weakens problem-solving ability. It hinders actual action and exhausts the people around you who are willing to listen. It correlates with anxiety, binge eating, alcohol abuse, and self-harm.
- Evidence grade: B
- Source: Nolen-Hoeksema, Wisco & Lyubomirsky (2008). Rethinking Rumination. Perspectives on Psychological Science. <https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1745-6924.2008.00088.x>
- Note: The review gives only direction, no numbers. "Switch to a different task" is the author's own practice. The literature supports only one sentence: diverting attention is better than continuing to go in circles. As for what to switch to, no one has compared.

### 15. Treat thoughts like "things will definitely get worse" as a symptom, not as fact
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=些 收益=中 口径=死亡率 -->
- Cost: Free. The hard part is treating such thoughts as a symptom rather than as fact.
- Plain-language summary: In a 20-year twin study, each tier increase in pessimism raised the probability of dying during the same period by about 13%, and cardiovascular death by about 20%. In the other direction, higher optimism scores did not bring corresponding benefit. So what to address is pessimism, not "insufficient positivity."
- Benefit: 2,978 Australian twins over age 50, followed for an average of 20 years, with 1,068 deaths during the period. Each 1 standard deviation increase on the pessimism scale raised mortality risk by about 13% (hazard ratio 1.134). The 95% CI is 1.065–1.207, P=8.85×10⁻⁵. Cardiovascular mortality about 20% higher (1.196, 1.045–1.368, P=0.0093). Not associated with cancer mortality. Optimism scores also showed no clear association with mortality.
- Evidence grade: B
- Source: Whitfield JB, Zhu G, Landers JG, Martin NG (2020). Pessimism is associated with greater all-cause and cardiovascular mortality, but optimism is not protective. Scientific Reports, 10, 12609. <https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-020-69388-y>
- Note: Controversy. This is observation only, no grouping. Some would say "actually they were sick first and then became pessimistic" — the author counters that if so, cancer mortality should also be associated. But other unmeasured factors may still exist. Optimism is not protective, so don't force yourself to "think positive." What to do is to not treat pessimistic predictions as accurate predictions of the future.

### 16. Reduce relationships that drain you; learn to refuse requests you don't want to accept
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=是 收益=中 口径=时间 -->
- Cost: Free. In the short term there will be social friction and possibly the loss of some relationships. Saying no takes considerable willpower.
- Plain-language summary: The damage from one draining person is greater than the benefit from one supportive person, and more consistent. So subtract first, add later.
- Benefit: A survey of 120 widowed women aged 60 to 89: the association of aversive interactions with psychological well-being was more consistent and stronger than that of supportive interactions. By this result, the damage from one draining person is greater than the benefit from one supportive person.
- Evidence grade: C
- Source: Rook (1984). The negative side of social interaction: Impact on psychological well-being. Journal of Personality and Social Psychology. <https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.46.5.1097>
- Note: The surveyed group is specific, and it's a one-time questionnaire, so cause and order can't be established. It measures well-being, not energy. Placing it in this section is the author's inference. There's no objective standard for which relationships count as "draining" — you have to judge for yourself.

### 17. Fix low-value decisions like what to wear and what to eat
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=小 口径=时间 -->
- Cost: Free. Up front, it takes a little time to set defaults.
- Plain-language summary: This entry has no hard evidence. The original experiment claiming "making choices depletes willpower" failed to replicate in two large-scale reproductions. Those two were one with 2,000 people and one with 3,000 people, run across multiple labs — the effect was essentially zero. Fixing what to wear and eat is still worth doing — the reason is saving those minutes and bouts of indecision, not "saving willpower."
- Benefit: Cannot be quantified. The original experiment claimed that making choices and self-control deplete a shared limited resource (ego depletion). But two later large-scale replications, both with pre-registered protocols and multiple labs reproducing as written, failed to produce the same result. One was 23 labs and 2,141 people, effect size d = 0.04, 95% CI [-0.07, 0.15] (confidence interval). The other was 36 labs and 3,531 people, d = 0.06. Differences too small to count.
- Evidence grade: C
- Source: Baumeister, Bratslavsky, Muraven & Tice (1998). Ego depletion: Is the active self a limited resource? Journal of Personality and Social Psychology. <https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.74.5.1252>; Hagger et al. (2016). A Multilab Preregistered Replication of the Ego-Depletion Effect. Perspectives on Psychological Science. <https://doi.org/10.1177/1745691616652873>; Vohs et al. (2021). A Multisite Preregistered Paradigmatic Test of the Ego-Depletion Effect. Psychological Science. <https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797621989733>
- Note: Controversy. "Decision fatigue" is treated as settled in popular books, but when others reproduce the original experiments, most fail to get the same result. It's still worth doing, because it costs almost nothing and the time saved is real. Don't count on it to improve your afternoon judgment.

### 18. When angry, leave the scene first; treat the other person as weather, not as an enemy
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=些 收益=中 口径=时间 -->
- Cost: Free. Hold back the urge to retort on the spot, walk away, decide later whether to deal with it. This takes some practice.
- Plain-language summary: Reframing what just happened, or putting yourself in the other person's shoes, both concretely lower the emotional intensity — the second works better. Suppressing the expression has no effect. Also, those who punish the offender ruminate on the matter longer afterward; those who don't retaliate move on faster. People overestimate in advance the satisfaction that revenge brings.
- Benefit: Pooled 306 experimental comparisons (meta-analysis): reframing the event (cognitive reappraisal) had effect size d = 0.36 on emotion. Putting yourself in the other's shoes d = 0.45. Distraction d = 0.27. Suppressing the expression had no effect (d = -0.04). Three other experiments showed: those who punished the offender thought about the offender longer afterward; those who didn't punish "turned the page" faster. People overestimated in advance the satisfaction from revenge.
- Evidence grade: B
- Source: Webb TL, Miles E, Sheeran P (2012). Dealing with feeling: A meta-analysis of the effectiveness of strategies derived from the process model of emotion regulation. Psychological Bulletin. <https://doi.org/10.1037/a0027600>; Carlsmith KM, Wilson TD, Gilbert DT (2008). The paradoxical consequences of revenge. Journal of Personality and Social Psychology. <https://doi.org/10.1037/a0012165>
- Note: The pooled-studies portion measures emotional intensity, not energy, with small-to-medium effects. The revenge portion has only one set of experiments, so graded B. The "empty boat" is a parable from Zhuangzi's "Mountain Tree" chapter: an empty boat drifting into yours doesn't make you angry; a boat with someone in it does — yet the impact is the same. This is an analogy, not evidence. If there's actual loss, take the legal route in Sections 8 and 9 — much cheaper than blowing up on the spot.

### 19. When mood is low, do the most cost-effective things first: move, get sun, sleep on time, talk to someone, call 12356
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=中 毅力=些 收益=大 口径=时间 -->
- Cost: Free, or very little. Brisk walking, jogging, or strength training a few times a week. Get sunlight during the day. Fix your schedule. Call one phone number when you can't see a way out.
- Plain-language summary: For depression, brisk walking and jogging work best, with yoga and strength training next; the higher the intensity, the more effective. Light therapy works very well for seasonal depression and also works for non-seasonal depression. Online self-help cognitive behavioral courses work; for more severe symptoms, having someone guide you is better. When you can't see a way out, call 12356 first.
- Benefit: Pooled 218 randomized controlled trials and 14,170 people (network meta-analysis). Controls were people doing some other activity. Compared with them, walking or jogging had effect size g = -0.62 (95% CI -0.80 to -0.45) on depression. Yoga -0.55, strength training -0.49. Higher intensity gave larger effects. Pooled light-therapy studies: seasonal depression effect size 0.84 (0.60 to 1.08), non-seasonal depression 0.53 (0.18 to 0.89). Another analysis pooled individual patient data across studies. It looked at internet-based cognitive behavioral therapy (online self-help courses). Both guided and unguided were better than control. For moderate-to-severe (PHQ-9 greater than 9), guided was better. 12356 is the national psychological assistance hotline, available no less than 18 hours a day.
- Evidence grade: A
- Source: Noetel M et al. (2024). Effect of exercise for depression: systematic review and network meta-analysis of randomised controlled trials. BMJ. <https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj-2023-075847>; Golden RN et al. (2005). The efficacy of light therapy in the treatment of mood disorders: a review and meta-analysis of the evidence. American Journal of Psychiatry. <https://doi.org/10.1176/appi.ajp.162.4.656>; Karyotaki E et al. (2021). Internet-Based Cognitive Behavioral Therapy for Depression: A Systematic Review and Individual Patient Data Network Meta-analysis. JAMA Psychiatry. <https://doi.org/10.1001/jamapsychiatry.2020.4364>; National Health Commission (2024). Notice on the Use of the "12356" National Unified Psychological Assistance Hotline Number (National Health Commission Medical Administration Letter [2024] No. 259). <https://www.gov.cn/zhengce/zhengceku/202412/content_6994470.htm>
- Note: These are adjunctive measures for mild-to-moderate depression. For moderate-to-severe, or with suicidal thoughts, seek medical care — first call 12356 (see Section 1, Entry 25). Most exercise trials have few subjects, and you can't blind who's exercising the way you can with drugs. The light-therapy studies only go up to 2003, also with few subjects. "Sleep on time" and "talk to someone" are the author's experience, no separate citation. Don't treat "move" as a demand on depressed people — even ten minutes of walking counts.

### 20. Treat police, doctors, and bank clerks as people working by rules, not as characters — what moves things forward is paperwork and deadlines, not emotion
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=些 收益=小 口径=时间 -->
- Cost: Free. What changes are your expectations and the order of actions. The first time, you have to resist the urge to "talk the other person into agreeing."
- Plain-language summary: Police, doctors, and bank clerks are people working to role rules, supervised by paperwork, deadlines, and oversight. Whether they're nice has little to do with whether your matter gets handled and how many days it takes. So get receipts, note deadlines, and appeal through proper channels — don't try to talk the other person into agreeing.
- Benefit: Change the actions you take with institutions: from "beg, get angry, retell grievances" to "get paperwork, note deadlines, appeal through channels." What's saved is the time of repeated communication and rumination. This is a one-time mindset adjustment, not daily time savings. What's verifiable are the deadlines themselves — they're all written in the documents. For public security cases, the case-handling period is no more than 30 days from acceptance; for major and complex cases, the next-higher public security organ may approve a 30-day extension. For cases with an accuser where the decision is not to file, the notice of non-filing must be delivered within 3 days. The accuser may apply for reconsideration within 7 days; the public security organ makes a decision within 30 days. If still dissatisfied, you may apply to the next-higher level for review within 7 days.
- Evidence grade: C
- Source: Ministry of Public Security (2020). Provisions on the Procedures for Handling Criminal Cases by Public Security Organs (Ministry of Public Security Decree No. 159 as amended, Articles 171, 178, 179). <http://www.gov.cn/gongbao/content/2020/content_5549884.htm>; Ministry of Public Security (2013/2019 amendment). Provisions on the Procedures for Handling Administrative Cases by Public Security Organs (Ministry of Public Security Decree No. 125, No. 149). <http://www.gov.cn/gongbao/content/2013/content_2321117.htm>
- Note: Graded C because: the judgment that "many people treat the person behind the window as a character, not as a worker" itself has no literature to cite. What's citable is only the rules half: deadlines, paperwork, and relief channels are all explicit. The numbers cited in this entry all come from the two Ministry of Public Security regulations cited in Section 8, Entry 39 (the process after calling police). Following the procedure cannot fix "the other person's bad attitude," nor guarantee a successful outcome — it only changes your expectations and your first step. Bank clerks and government-service windows are the same: their operations leave traces, require review, and mistakes are subject to accountability. So what to do is keep receipts, ask for transaction slips, and complain through channels — don't guess at "will he cheat me." Lawful priorities are written out in the open — for example, emergency departments triage by severity, not by arrival order (Section 24, Entry 8, emergency triage). To thank a doctor who saved you, write a thank-you letter and complete a satisfaction survey, see Section 24, Entry 12. "Sending something to make them pay more attention" to law-enforcement officers is the crime of bribery, and is explicitly an aggravated form, see Section 8, Entry 40. When you encounter clear differential treatment, complain — don't take it as an unwritten rule to imitate.

### 21. Don't treat "how others are doing" as required daily reading — set limits on or turn off apps that show peers' updates
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=是 收益=大 口径=时间 -->
- Cost: Free. Setting app limits or deactivating accounts takes a few minutes. The hard part is not opening them afterward — long-term resistance to inertia.
- Plain-language summary: People who habitually compare themselves online with those doing better tend to have worse mood and lower self-esteem. Those who deactivated Facebook for four weeks had small improvements in well-being, but also a drop in awareness of current events. Those who limited each of three social platforms to 10 minutes per day had reduced loneliness and depression after three weeks.
- Benefit: A pooled analysis (meta-analysis): 54 independent samples, 94 effect sizes, N = 36,583, using a three-level random-effects model. Online upward social comparison and psychological maladjustment had an average correlation r = 0.330 (95% CI 0.289–0.370, the confidence interval). Split by outcome. Fear of being looked down on, a social-evaluative negative emotion, 0.438. Anxiety 0.382. Depression 0.306. Low well-being 0.268. Low self-esteem 0.263. Age, cultural background, and year of data collection did not affect the strength of the association. Long-term cohort studies gave no stronger results than cross-sectional surveys. There was also a randomized controlled trial with 2,743 U.S. Facebook users. 61% were willing to accept deactivation for less than 102 USD. These people were randomly assigned to a four-week deactivation group or a control group. Deactivation freed up 60 minutes per day for the average subject. Subjective well-being index rose 0.09 standard deviations. Sub-items: life satisfaction 0.12, anxiety 0.10, depression 0.09, happiness 0.08. These remained significant after correcting for multiple comparisons. The authors offer a comparison: psychological interventions that make people more positive. Such interventions raise subjective well-being by about +0.34 SD and reduce depression by about -0.23 SD. So this effect is about 25% to 40% of standard psychological interventions. The simultaneous cost: current-events knowledge index fell 0.19 SD, and political-polarization index fell 0.16 SD. After the experiment, the deactivation group's Facebook use was still 12 minutes/day lower than control (0.31 SD, a 23% reduction). Another randomized controlled trial, 143 undergraduates randomized into two groups. One limited Facebook, Instagram, and Snapchat each to 10 minutes per day; the other used as usual. After three weeks, the limited group showed clear drops in loneliness and depression. Both groups had lower anxiety and fear of missing out than at baseline.
- Evidence grade: A
- Source: Lei Y, Hu S, Sun Y, Zheng L (2026). "Looking up" linked to feeling down: a meta-analysis of online upward social comparison and psychological maladjustment. Frontiers in Psychology. <https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2026.1825169>; Allcott H, Braghieri L, Eichmeyer S, Gentzkow M (2020). The Welfare Effects of Social Media. American Economic Review, 110(3), 629–676. <https://doi.org/10.1257/aer.20190658>; Allcott H, Braghieri L, Eichmeyer S, Gentzkow M (2019). The Welfare Effects of Social Media. NBER Working Paper No. 25514 (the specific numbers cited here are verified against this version; the published-version abstract does not contain numbers). <https://www.nber.org/papers/w25514>; Hunt MG, Marx R, Lipson C, Young J (2018). No More FOMO: Limiting Social Media Decreases Loneliness and Depression. Journal of Social and Clinical Psychology, 37(10), 751–768. <https://doi.org/10.1521/jscp.2018.37.10.751>
- Note: The "correlation" half only indicates the two co-occur, not which causes which — the authors themselves say more long-term cohort studies are needed. The randomized half can speak to causation, but the subjects were Facebook users around the 2018 U.S. midterm elections, so transferring to Chinese platforms is a hard fit. Hunt's had only 143 undergraduates, and only that one study. Effect sizes are all small — don't count on closing the app to fix mood problems. What needs blocking is the "see how others are doing" kind of use; connecting with family and friends, looking up information, and job hunting don't count. If you really can't hold back, start by turning off push, "people you may know," and home-feed recommendations. Total screen-time accounting is in Section 4, Entries 15 and 16 (hard cap on short videos, don't read scrolling news). What to do first when mood is low is in Entry 19 of this section (move, get sun, sleep on time). Buying things to recover a sense of identity is in Section 6, Entry 23. Spending more to "move up a tier" is in Section 6, Entry 24.

### 22. When you feel "everyone saw me embarrass myself," divide that estimate by two
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=些 收益=小 口径=时间 -->
- Cost: Free. Just ask yourself one more question when nervous: did they really see?
- Plain-language summary: Wearing an embarrassing T-shirt into a room with people, wearers thought nearly half of observers could identify the figure on the shirt — actually fewer than a quarter. Wearing a T-shirt they themselves considered decent, the estimated number was six times the actual. After conversations, people also generally underestimate how much the other person liked them. So when you feel "everyone remembers my embarrassment," halving it still overestimates.
- Benefit: Experiment 1: participants wore a T-shirt with Barry Manilow's face and entered a room with other participants. Wearers on average estimated 46% of bystanders could correctly identify the person on the shirt. Bystanders' actual accuracy was 23% — the estimate was exactly twice the actual. Subtracting actual from estimated per session, the average overestimate was 23 percentage points (95% CI 9–38, the confidence interval). Excluding sessions with only two or three bystanders, it was 27 percentage points (11–43). Experiment 2: participants wore a T-shirt they themselves chose and considered positive. The average estimate was 48%, with bystanders' actual accuracy 8% — six times. Another series of studies had three scenarios: strangers paired to converse, college freshman roommates, and adult workshops. The result was that people systematically underestimated how much their conversation partners liked them, and how much the partners enjoyed the conversation. The authors called this the "liking gap." It existed across conversations of varying length and persisted for months between freshman roommates.
- Evidence grade: B
- Source: Gilovich T, Medvec VH, Savitsky K (2000). The spotlight effect in social judgment: An egocentric bias in estimates of the salience of one's own actions and appearance. Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 78(2), 211–222. <https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.78.2.211>; Boothby EJ, Cooney G, Sandstrom GM, Clark MS (2018). The Liking Gap in Conversations: Do People Like Us More Than We Think? Psychological Science, 29(11), 1742–1756. <https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797618783714>
- Note: Graded B because: these are small lab and campus studies, with scenarios of wearing clothes and chatting with strangers. Transferring to "being gossiped about by colleagues" or "saying something wrong in a meeting" has no direct evidence. The numbers only indicate the direction and rough magnitude of overestimation, not coefficients to apply. Others' opinions aren't all unimportant either. In situations where you're actually being evaluated — interviews, defenses, appraisals, medical checkup reports — the evaluation is real; prepare as needed. What needs discounting is just the everyday "everyone is looking at me" feeling; what to address there is your own estimation bias.

### 23. Treat "others demand I be perfect" as a symptom, not as fact
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=些 收益=小 口径=死亡率 -->
- Cost: Free. The hard part is treating such thoughts as something to address rather than as an accurate measurement of others' actual demands.
- Plain-language summary: People who constantly feel "those around me demand I be perfect, and if I fall short I'll be rejected" are more likely to have suicidal thoughts. The relationship between "I demand perfection of myself" and suicidal thoughts is much weaker, only about a third of the former. Subtracting pre-existing thoughts, the former feeling still correlates with later increases in suicidal thoughts, though only modestly.
- Benefit: A pooled analysis (meta-analysis): 45 studies, N = 11,747, with populations of undergraduates, medical students, community adults, and psychiatric patients, using a random-effects model. Below are weighted mean correlations with suicidal thoughts. Perfectionistic concerns r = .28 (95% CI .24–.32, the confidence interval; 42 studies, 7,936 people). Perfectionistic concerns is a composite that includes socially prescribed perfectionism, concern over mistakes, doubts about actions, and discrepancy between reality and standards. Among these, socially prescribed perfectionism alone was also r = .28 (.25–.32, 30 studies, 3,640 people). Perfectionistic striving r = .10 (.07–.13, 31 studies, 4,588 people) — a composite of self-oriented perfectionism and personal standards. Parental criticism r = .20 (.11–.29). Looking at suicide attempts: perfectionistic concerns r = .12 (.07–.17, 15 studies, 5,275 people). Socially prescribed perfectionism r = .19 (.08–.29, 8 studies, 689 people). The longitudinal portion: after subtracting pre-existing suicidal thoughts, socially prescribed perfectionism still covaried with suicidal thoughts at follow-up, modestly. Self-oriented and other-oriented perfectionism showed no association.
- Evidence grade: B
- Source: Smith MM, Sherry SB, Chen S, Saklofske DH, Mushquash C, Flett GL, Hewitt PL (2018). The perniciousness of perfectionism: A meta-analytic review of the perfectionism-suicide relationship. Journal of Personality, 86(3), 522–542. <https://doi.org/10.1111/jopy.12333>
- Note: Graded B because: most included studies are one-time questionnaires, so cause and order can't be established. Correlation strength is moderate (r about .28), explaining only a few to ten percent of variance. What's measured is suicidal thoughts and attempts, not death itself, so the benefit magnitude is graded "small" per the rule of only surrogate endpoints. The authors themselves note that perfectionistic concerns and suicide attempts appear to have publication bias — meaning studies with less favorable results weren't published. After imputing the missing studies, the direction of conclusion did not change. High standards themselves are not the problem, and there's no need to lower your standards. The distinction lies in where the standards come from, and whether falling short makes you feel "I am worthless as a person." Like the pessimistic predictions in Entry 15 (treat "things will definitely get worse" as a symptom), this is something that can be treated as a symptom. With suicidal thoughts, first call 12356 and move lethal means away — see Section 1, Entry 25. For what to do when someone close to you voices such thoughts, see Section 8, Entry 15. For childhood depression screening, see Section 30, Entry 8.

### 24. When angry, don't vent by breaking things, punching a heavy bag, or going for a run — first slow the body down
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=些 收益=中 口径=时间 -->
- Cost: Free. In the heat of anger, do something for a few minutes that slows the heartbeat: breathe slowly, sit down, be still. The hard part is that what you most want to do at that moment is vent.
- Plain-language summary: Punching a heavy bag, smashing things, going for a run — these arousal-increasing venting methods do not reduce anger overall. Calming methods like slow breathing, mindfulness, and meditation noticeably reduce anger and the urge to act.
- Benefit: A pooled analysis of 154 studies (meta-analysis), totaling 184 samples and 10,189 people. Calming methods like deep breathing, mindfulness, and meditation reduced anger and aggression. Effect size g = -0.63, 95% CI -0.82 to -0.43 (the confidence interval). This result was stable across different genders, races, ages, and cultures. Arousal-increasing methods like punching a heavy bag, jogging, and cycling were overall ineffective. Effect size g = -0.02, 95% CI -0.13 to 0.09. The authors concluded: venting anger, going for a run — neither is an effective way to reduce anger.
- Evidence grade: A
- Source: Kjærvik SL, Bushman BJ (2024). A meta-analytic review of anger management activities that increase or decrease arousal: What fuels or douses rage? Clinical Psychology Review. <https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cpr.2024.102414>
- Note: What's measured is anger and aggression, not energy. The metric is recorded as time, by the author's classification: one less argument means one less mess to clean up. Methods usable on the spot are in Section 22, Entry 9 (cyclic sighing), and Entry 18 of this section (leave the scene when angry). Running isn't useless. Long-term, it works for low mood, see Section 22, Entry 7 (walk or run when mood is low). It's just not suitable for cooling anger on the spot. When angry, don't drive, don't send messages, don't make decisions. If someone is hitting you, leave first and call police. What to handle then is not your emotions — see Section 8, Entry 43 (if you're being subjected to domestic violence).

### 25. If something is weighing on you, you can write down your thoughts and feelings for several days running — but don't expect it to cure illness
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=小 口径=时间 -->
- Cost: Free. The common practice in studies is 3 to 4 sessions of 15 to 20 minutes each. Write about something that hurts you, write your deepest thoughts and feelings.
- Plain-language summary: Writing down bad experiences gives a small average benefit, but tiny. Pooled conclusions are also inconsistent — two pooled analyses found no effect. It costs almost nothing, so you can try it. If emotional problems are serious, don't substitute it for seeing a doctor.
- Benefit: One analysis pooled 146 randomized studies (meta-analysis). Participants wrote or spoke about things important to them. The average effect was positive and significant but small, effect size r = .075. Another pooled analysis restricted to randomized controlled trials pooled 30 trials — neither physical nor psychological health showed significant effects. Sixteen randomized trials in cancer patients and survivors pooled: no significant effect on psychological, physical, or quality-of-life outcomes. The psychological effect was g = 0.04, 95% CI -0.06 to 0.14 (the confidence interval).
- Evidence grade: B
- Source: Frattaroli J (2006). Experimental disclosure and its moderators: a meta-analysis. Psychological Bulletin. <https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.132.6.823>; Mogk C, Otte S, Reinhold-Hurley B, Kröner-Herwig B (2006). Health effects of expressive writing on stressful or traumatic experiences - a meta-analysis. Psycho-Social Medicine. <https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC2736499/>; Zachariae R, O'Toole MS (2015). The effect of expressive writing intervention on psychological and physical health outcomes in cancer patients--a systematic review and meta-analysis. Psycho-Oncology. <https://doi.org/10.1002/pon.3802>
- Note: Controversy. The supporting side is the pooled analysis of 146 studies with a small but positive effect. The opposing side is two pooled analyses restricted to RCTs, neither finding significant effects. The "several sessions, each how long" in the cost column comes from the original experimental design and is described in Mogk's pooled-analysis abstract. It costs almost nothing and takes little time, and can be done alone. When you don't want to talk to someone, or have no one to talk to, you can use it. What's measured is not energy; the metric recorded as time is the author's classification. If writing makes you feel worse and worse, stop and call 12356 instead, see Section 1, Entry 25 (12356).
