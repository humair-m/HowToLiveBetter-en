# 34. Don't Let Household Common Medicines Cause Harm

Antipyretics, painkillers, cold medicines, stomach medicines, anti-diarrheals — many can be purchased at a pharmacy yourself. Being available without a prescription does not mean any way of taking them is fine. This section does not cover which medicine to take for which disease — it covers only the few things where a single action can avoid a serious outcome.

All entries are calculated by mortality, including endpoints such as liver failure, gastric bleeding, and neonatal kidney failure. The benefit is mostly "one fewer serious incident"; research does not provide figures for "how many percent fewer incidents occur if done." The benefit magnitude is judged by the severity of the consequence, and each entry's note explains the basis for the rating.

Things already covered elsewhere are only pointed to here. What to do first after mistakenly taking medicine or cleaning agent, see Section 13, Entry 20 (accidental ingestion). For taking chronic-disease medications as prescribed to completion, see Section 16, Entry 1. For online purchase of prescription drugs requiring prior prescription review, see Section 28, Entry 6 (going to the hospital for a weight-loss prescription).

The beneficiaries of this section's entries are you and your family, in the highest two tiers.

### 1. Before taking two cold medicines or painkillers together, check the ingredients list — acetaminophen (paracetamol) should appear in only one
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=大 口径=死亡率 -->
- Cost: Free of charge. Glance at the ingredients list on each box — a minute or two.
- Plain-language summary: Many cold medicines contain acetaminophen; taking two boxes together means a double dose, and overdose injures the liver. In the United States, of acute liver failure patients caused by acetaminophen, nearly 30% died before a transplant could be arranged. Domestic package inserts recommend a daily maximum of 2 grams, and alcohol should be avoided during use.
- Benefit: 22 US hospitals consecutively enrolled 662 cases of acute liver failure over 6 years. Of these, 275 (42%) were caused by acetaminophen; the median ingested dose was 24 grams. Among these 275, 131 (48%) were unintentional overdoses, not intentional. Of those with unintentional overdose, 38% had simultaneously taken two or more products containing this ingredient. Among these 275, 27% died before a transplant could be arranged, and 8% underwent liver transplantation. The National Medical Products Administration in 2020 required that over-the-counter package inserts state: overdose can cause severe liver injury, with a recommended daily maximum of no more than 2 grams. The package insert must also state that combination with other products containing acetaminophen or other antipyretic-analgesic drugs should be avoided whenever possible. US regulations require this class of OTC drug to state three liver-harm scenarios as the first warning: exceeding the daily maximum; taking with other acetaminophen-containing products; and drinking 3 or more alcoholic beverages per day during use.
- Evidence grade: B
- Source: Larson AM, Polson J, Fontana RJ, et al.; Acute Liver Failure Study Group (2005). Acetaminophen-induced acute liver failure: results of a United States multicenter, prospective study. Hepatology, 42(6):1364-1372. <https://doi.org/10.1002/hep.20948>; National Medical Products Administration announcement on revising the package inserts of acetaminophen immediate-release and extended-release preparations (2020 No. 15) and Appendix 2: Package Insert Revision Requirements for Acetaminophen Immediate-Release OTC Preparations (reproduced by the Hunan Provincial Medical Products Administration). <http://mpa.hunan.gov.cn/mpa/xxgk/tzgg/wjtz/202003/t20200311_11809335.html>; 21 CFR 201.326(a)(1)(iii)(A) Liver warning. <https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-21/chapter-I/subchapter-C/part-201/subpart-G/section-201.326>
- Note: Acetaminophen is also called paracetamol. Among the generic names of compound cold medicines, those containing "Anfen" (e.g., Anfen Weima, Anka Huangmin) contain this ingredient. If unsure, take all the boxes to a pharmacist. Rated B because the US figures come from case registries without controls, so the number of incidents avoided by checking the ingredients list cannot be calculated. The benefit is rated "Large" by consequence: nearly 30% of acute liver failure cases resulted in direct death.

### 2. For a child's fever, do not use aspirin, nimesulide, or metamizole sodium (analgin); cold medicines containing metamizole should also not be given to minors
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=大 口径=死亡率 -->
- Cost: Free of charge. When buying antipyretics, glance at the generic name and ingredients list.
- Plain-language summary: For reducing a child's fever, do not use any of these three. Aspirin is associated with Reye's syndrome, a disease that damages the brain and liver; US statistics show that about 30% of affected children died, and after warnings were issued, cases dropped by more than 90%. Nimesulide is prohibited for children under 12; metamizole is prohibited for those under 18.
- Benefit: Reye's syndrome causes encephalopathy and fatty degeneration of the liver, typically after influenza or chickenpox. In US national surveillance from 1981 to 1997, 1,207 cases were reported in those under 18. Salicylates were detected in the blood of 82% of affected children — these are the ingredients of drugs like aspirin. The overall case fatality rate was 31%. Warnings about salicylate-class drugs began in 1980. That year 555 cases were reported — the peak — and from 1987 onward, no more than 36 cases per year. The State Food and Drug Administration in 2011 prohibited oral nimesulide preparations for children under 12. The National Medical Products Administration in 2020 prohibited metamizole tablets for adolescents and children under 18. Compound metamizole tablets (Qinghao An Nai Jin Pian, Zhong Gan Ling Pian, and Zhong Gan Ling Jiao Nang) containing metamizole are likewise prohibited for those under 18.
- Evidence grade: B
- Source: Belay ED, Bresee JS, Holman RC, Khan AS, Shahriari A, Schonberger LB (1999). Reye's syndrome in the United States from 1981 through 1997. New England Journal of Medicine, 340(18):1377-1382. <https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJM199905063401801>; Notice on Strengthening the Use Management of Oral Nimesulide Preparations (Guo Shi Yao Jian An [2011] No. 209). <https://www.nmpa.gov.cn/xxgk/fgwj/gzwj/gzwjyp/20110515145101552.html>; National Medical Products Administration announcement on revising the package inserts of metamizole-related products (2020 No. 34) and Appendices 1 to 3. <https://www.nmpa.gov.cn/directory/web/nmpa/xxgk/ggtg/ypggtg/ypshmshxdgg/20200317171201660.html>
- Note: Metamizole is not the first-choice drug for adults either. Its package insert states that it may cause agranulocytosis, aplastic anemia, and anaphylactic shock, and should only be used short-term when the condition is acute and severe and no other effective drug is available. "Zhong Gan Ling" sounds like an ordinary cold medicine by name, but it contains metamizole. For which antipyretic a child should use and the dose by body weight, ask a pediatrician or pharmacist. For fevers in children under 3 months, go directly to the hospital — see Section 20, Entry 8 (go directly to the hospital at 38 °C). Rated B because Reye's syndrome figures come from surveillance reports, and the temporal association between case decline and warning issuance is not a controlled trial. The benefit is rated "Large" because cases dropped from 555 per year to under 36 after the warning.

### 3. For those over 60, with prior gastric bleeding, or taking anticoagulants or steroids, ask a doctor before taking ibuprofen-class painkillers
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=大 口径=死亡率 -->
- Cost: Free of charge. Ask a doctor or pharmacist once.
- Plain-language summary: Painkillers such as ibuprofen, naproxen, and aspirin harm the stomach. In trials of high-dose use, the risk of gastric bleeding or perforation in ibuprofen users was about 4 times that of non-users. The risk is higher in older adults, those with prior gastric bleeding, those taking anticoagulants or steroids concurrently, and those who drink alcohol daily.
- Benefit: This class of drugs is called non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs); common ones include ibuprofen, naproxen, and diclofenac. A meta-analysis pooled 280 randomized trials of these drugs versus placebo, totaling 124,513 people. Compared with placebo, the risk of upper gastrointestinal perforation, obstruction, or bleeding: ibuprofen RR 3.97 (95% CI 2.22–7.10, about 4 times); naproxen 4.22 (2.71–6.56); diclofenac 1.89 (1.16–3.09). All drugs in this class approximately double the risk of heart failure. US regulations require this class of OTC drug to state a gastric bleeding warning. The warning lists six categories of people at higher risk: those over 60; those with prior stomach ulcer or bleeding; those taking anticoagulants or steroids; those taking other drugs in this class concurrently; those drinking 3 or more alcoholic beverages per day during use; and those taking more or for longer than the package insert directs.
- Evidence grade: A
- Source: Coxib and traditional NSAID Trialists' (CNT) Collaboration; Bhala N, Emberson J, Merhi A, et al. (2013). Vascular and upper gastrointestinal effects of non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs: meta-analyses of individual participant data from randomised trials. Lancet, 382(9894):769-779. <https://doi.org/10.1016/S0140-6736(13)60900-9>; 21 CFR 201.326(a)(2)(iii)(A) Stomach bleeding warning. <https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-21/chapter-I/subchapter-C/part-201/subpart-G/section-201.326>
- Note: The paper's conclusions concern high-dose use. The risk of occasional one-or-two-day use at the labeled dose is not separately quantified in this paper. Many compound cold medicines also contain ingredients in this class, and combining them with painkillers duplicates the dose — check the ingredients list first. If a doctor has put you on long-term low-dose aspirin to prevent clotting, do not stop on your own, and do not add another painkiller on your own.

### 4. For children under 2 with a cold, do not self-administer compound cold medicines and cough medicines
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=中 口径=死亡率 -->
- Cost: Free of charge, also saving the cost of the medicine.
- Plain-language summary: The National Medical Products Administration requires 14 children's compound cold medicines to carry a warning: parental self-administration for children under 2 is not recommended, and they should be used under a doctor's or pharmacist's guidance. In trials on children, cough medicines and allergy medicines were no more effective than placebo.
- Benefit: The National Medical Products Administration's 2021 No. 57 announcement revised the package inserts of 14 varieties, including Anfen Ma Mei oral solution, Xiao'er Anfen Huang Na Min granules, and Xiao'er Anfen Wan An granules. The new warning language reads: "It is not recommended that parents or guardians self-administer this product to infants under 2 years of age; it should be used under the guidance of a physician or pharmacist." Precautions added: "Use strictly in accordance with the dosage specified in the package insert to avoid overdose." Also added: "Avoid combination use with cold medicines containing the same or similar active ingredients." A Cochrane systematic review included 29 randomized controlled trials, 10 in children. In pediatric trials, none of these drug classes was better than placebo: cough medicines, antihistamines (allergy ingredients), antihistamines plus decongestants (nasal-clearing ingredients), and cough medicines plus bronchodilators. 21 trials reported adverse events, with preparations containing antihistamines and dextromethorphan having more adverse events.
- Evidence grade: B
- Source: National Medical Products Administration announcement on revising the package inserts of 14 varieties including Anfen Ma Mei oral solution (2021 No. 57) and appendices. <https://www.nmpa.gov.cn/directory/web/nmpa/xxgk/ggtg/ypggtg/ypshmshxdgg/20210423105404141.html>; Smith SM, Schroeder K, Fahey T (2014). Over-the-counter (OTC) medications for acute cough in children and adults in community settings. Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews, (11):CD001831. <https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD001831.pub5>
- Note: Many of these 14 have "Anfen" in their name and also contain acetaminophen; combining them with an antipyretic duplicates the dose — see Entry 1 of this section (acetaminophen). One trial in the review found honey better than placebo, but honey should not be given to infants under 1 year — see Section 20, Entry 6 (honey). Rated B because the trials in each drug category in the review are few, and the authors did not pool a number. The benefit is rated "Medium" by judgment: this class of drugs has no proven benefit in children, and the risks are mainly overdose and ingredient duplication.

### 5. After 20 weeks of pregnancy, do not self-administer ibuprofen-class painkillers
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=中 口径=死亡率 -->
- Cost: Free of charge.
- Plain-language summary: After about 20 weeks of pregnancy, most of the amniotic fluid is produced by the fetal kidneys. Taking ibuprofen-class drugs at this time may injure the fetal kidneys and reduce amniotic fluid. Among US reports received, there were cases of neonatal death. Low-dose aspirin prescribed by a doctor is not in this category.
- Benefit: The US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) issued a safety communication in October 2020. Use of NSAIDs after about 20 weeks of pregnancy may cause fetal kidney problems, leading to oligohydramnios. The warning covers both prescription and OTC drugs, including aspirin, ibuprofen, naproxen, diclofenac, and celecoxib. The FDA reviewed adverse event reports through July 2017 and identified 35 cases of oligohydramnios or neonatal renal insufficiency, all with serious outcomes. Of these, 5 neonatal deaths occurred, all with neonatal renal failure. In the literature, most cases of oligohydramnios resolved within 72 hours to 6 days after drug discontinuation. The FDA advises pregnant women not to use this class of drugs after 20 weeks unless a doctor specifically so advises. Low-dose aspirin (81 mg) prescribed by a doctor for specific pregnancy conditions is not subject to this restriction. For pain relief and fever reduction, the FDA mentions that other drugs such as acetaminophen may be used — consult a pharmacist or doctor first.
- Evidence grade: B
- Source: U.S. Food and Drug Administration (2020-10-15). FDA recommends avoiding use of NSAIDs in pregnancy at 20 weeks or later because they can result in low amniotic fluid. Drug Safety Communication. <https://www.fda.gov/drugs/drug-safety-and-availability/fda-recommends-avoiding-use-nsaids-pregnancy-20-weeks-or-later-because-they-can-result-low-amniotic>
- Note: The FDA notes that many OTC drugs for colds, flu, and insomnia also contain ingredients in this class — check the ingredients list. The original OTC drug labels in the US warned only of use in the last 3 months of pregnancy; this communication moved the cutoff earlier to around 20 weeks. For low-dose aspirin to prevent preeclampsia, see Section 27, Entry 5 (low-dose aspirin). Rated B because the figures come from adverse event reports; how many people took the drug without incident is unknown. The benefit is rated "Medium" by judgment: the outcomes are severe but occur infrequently.

### 6. For acid reflux and heartburn bought as omeprazole OTC, take for no more than 7 days; for swallowing difficulty, vomiting blood, or black stool, go directly to the hospital
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=中 口径=死亡率 -->
- Cost: Free of charge. Follow the package insert's number of days.
- Plain-language summary: Omeprazole enteric-coated tablets have been available without prescription since 2022, but the package insert states that self-use should not exceed 7 days, and that it should not be taken again within two months. Do not self-administer for difficulty or pain in swallowing, vomiting blood, bloody stool, or black stool — these may signal a serious condition. This drug may also mask symptoms of stomach cancer and delay diagnosis.
- Benefit: The National Medical Products Administration's 2022 No. 68 announcement reclassified omeprazole enteric-coated tablets from prescription to OTC and simultaneously published a model package insert. The model insert states the indication as "short-term relief of heartburn and acid regurgitation symptoms caused by excess stomach acid." Precautions state: use no more than 7 days; if symptoms are not relieved, consult a physician or pharmacist. Do not take again within two months; if symptoms recur, seek medical attention immediately. Do not use for difficulty or pain in swallowing, vomiting blood, bloody stool, or black stool — these may be signs of a serious condition. When alarm symptoms such as marked weight loss, recurrent vomiting, difficulty swallowing, vomiting blood, or black stool appear, malignant tumor must first be ruled out, because treatment may mask symptoms and delay diagnosis. Those aged 55 or above with new or changed symptoms should consult a doctor. Those taking clopidogrel should avoid concomitant use of omeprazole.
- Evidence grade: B
- Source: National Medical Products Administration announcement on converting omeprazole enteric-coated tablets from prescription to OTC (2022 No. 68) and Appendix 2: Model OTC Package Insert. <https://www.nmpa.gov.cn/yaopin/ypggtg/20220826171517123.html>
- Note: Clopidogrel is an anti-clotting drug commonly taken by those who have had a stent placed or a heart attack. Omeprazole weakens its effect — those taking it should ask a doctor first. Rated B because these are package insert regulations, with no research calculating how many fewer incidents occur by following them. The benefit is rated "Medium" by judgment: it prevents late-stage gastric bleeding and gastric cancer, but most acid reflux and heartburn are not serious.

### 7. For an ordinary cold, do not ask a doctor for antibiotics
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=中 口径=死亡率 -->
- Cost: Free of charge, also saving the cost of the medicine.
- Plain-language summary: Antibiotics do not make a cold resolve faster than without them. In adults who took them, the risk of adverse effects was more than twice that of those who did not. For purulent nasal discharge under 10 days, antibiotics offer no clear benefit either.
- Benefit: A Cochrane systematic review pooled 6 randomized trials, 1,147 people. Comparing antibiotics with placebo for the common cold, the proportion not recovered or with persistent symptoms showed no significant difference (RR 0.83, 95% CI 0.60–1.14, not significant). The risk of adverse events was 1.8 times that of the placebo group (95% CI 1.01–3.21). Among adults, 2.62 times (1.32–5.18); among children, no increase (0.91, 0.51–1.63). For acute purulent rhinitis with purulent nasal discharge under 10 days, antibiotics offered no clear benefit either (RR 0.73, 0.47–1.13). Adverse events were more frequent (RR 1.46, 1.10–1.94, about 46% higher). The authors conclude: neither condition warrants routine antibiotic use.
- Evidence grade: A
- Source: Kenealy T, Arroll B (2025). Antibiotics for the common cold and acute purulent rhinitis. Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews, 11:CD000247. <https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD000247.pub4>
- Note: This version was republished in 2025; the abstract states that the literature search ran through 2013. Antibiotics are prescription drugs domestically; pharmacies must sell them with a prescription, and leftover antibiotics at home should not be self-administered. The benefit is rated "Medium" by judgment: it prevents adverse events, not death.

### 8. For diarrhea, first rehydrate with oral rehydration salts; do not give anti-diarrheal medicines to children under 5
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=少 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=中 口径=死亡率 -->
- Cost: A box of oral rehydration salts costs a few to over ten yuan; keep one at home.
- Plain-language summary: For diarrhea, the priority is rehydration, not stopping the diarrhea. Mix oral rehydration salts with water per the package insert — do not substitute juice drinks, which are too sweet and worsen dehydration. Children under 5 should not be given anti-diarrheals such as loperamide; severe cases can cause intestinal obstruction, which can be fatal.
- Benefit: The World Health Organization's diarrhea treatment manual states that "anti-diarrhea" medicines and antiemetics offer no real benefit for acute or persistent diarrhea in children. They do not prevent dehydration or improve nutrition. Some have dangerous and sometimes fatal side effects and must not be given to children under 5. Loperamide and other drugs that slow intestinal motility may cause severe paralytic ileus, which can be fatal, and may prolong infection. The manual also states that drinks with too much sugar draw water from the body into the intestines, causing hypernatremic dehydration — for example, soft drinks and commercial juice drinks. The WHO manual recommends a reduced-osmolarity formula with total osmolarity of 245 mOsm/L. Compared with the old formula, it reduced unscheduled IV fluids by 33%. A Cochrane review pooled 8 trials with similar results (OR 0.59, 95% CI 0.45–0.79, about a 40% reduction).
- Evidence grade: A
- Source: World Health Organization (2005). The treatment of diarrhoea: a manual for physicians and other senior health workers, 4th revision. <https://iris.who.int/handle/10665/43209>; Hahn S, Kim S, Garner P (2002). Reduced osmolarity oral rehydration solution for treating dehydration caused by acute diarrhoea in children. Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews, (1):CD002847. <https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD002847>
- Note: When buying, check the osmolarity on the package insert and select a reduced-osmolarity formula. The manual notes that bloody stool in children is most often bacterial dysentery, requiring a doctor's antibiotic treatment — not just rehydration at home. The benefit is rated "Medium" by judgment: the figures compare two rehydration salts, not drinking versus not drinking.

### 9. Painkillers should not be taken more than 15 days per month, and compound painkillers not more than 10 days — otherwise the headache itself may be caused by the medication
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=些 收益=中 口径=死亡率 -->
- Cost: Free of charge. Track the days you take medication, and resist taking it casually.
- Plain-language summary: For chronic headaches with daily painkiller use, the medication may cause more headaches. The international headache classification calls this medication-overuse headache. Plain painkillers taken 15 or more days per month, or compound painkillers taken 10 or more days per month, for more than 3 consecutive months, meets the threshold. Most people improve after stopping the overused medication.
- Benefit: The International Classification of Headache Disorders, 3rd edition, by the International Headache Society, gives diagnostic criteria. For those with a pre-existing headache disorder, headaches on 15 or more days per month, with regular overuse of headache medication for more than 3 months. The overuse threshold for non-opioid painkillers such as acetaminophen, aspirin, and ibuprofen is 15 or more days per month. For compound painkillers, the threshold is 10 or more days per month. "Compound painkiller" refers to combinations of two or more drugs with analgesic effect, or those with auxiliary ingredients such as caffeine — for example, acetaminophen plus codeine. When several non-opioid painkillers are taken in rotation, the days are summed. The classification notes that epidemiological evidence from multiple countries shows that among those with headaches 15 or more days per month, more than half fall into this category. Most patients improve after stopping the overused medication, and preventive headache treatment becomes more effective.
- Evidence grade: B
- Source: Headache Classification Committee of the International Headache Society (2018). The International Classification of Headache Disorders, 3rd edition. Cephalalgia, 38(1):1-211. <https://doi.org/10.1177/0333102417738202>; ICHD-3 online version 8.2 Medication-overuse headache (including 8.2.3, 8.2.5). <https://ichd-3.org/8-headache-attributed-to-a-substance-or-its-withdrawal/8-2-medication-overuse-headache-moh/>
- Note: Painkiller names containing "Ka" (coffee / caffeine) usually have caffeine added and count as compound, at the 10-day threshold. If the number of headache days per month keeps rising, see a neurologist rather than just increasing medication. Rated B because the classification gives diagnostic thresholds, not a controlled trial. The benefit is rated "Medium" by judgment: it buys back headache-free days, with no mortality involved.
