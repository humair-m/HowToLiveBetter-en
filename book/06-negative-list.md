# 6. Negative List

This section collects things that "look cheap and effective." Open up the original research, and their benefit is close to zero, equal to zero, sometimes even negative. The ordering rests on two things: how much money people spend on them, and how hard the supporting evidence is. The closer to the front, the sooner you should stop.

### 1. Don't take multivitamins for longevity or cardiovascular disease prevention
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=中 口径=金钱 -->
- Cost: About 0.5 to 2 yuan a day, 200 to 700 yuan a year. Taking it costs no time, just one pill. Over time you develop dependence, feeling like you've missed something on a day you don't take it.
- Plain-language summary: 14,000 male physicians took multivitamins for eleven years. Major cardiovascular events and deaths were the same as in those who didn't take them. Pool 9 trials and 50,000+ people together, and no effect on death shows up. Not buying saves 200 to 700 yuan a year. People confirmed to be deficient in a specific nutrient and pregnant women taking folic acid are not covered by this entry.
- Benefit: The PHS II trial enrolled 14,641 male physicians and followed them for 11.2 years (this is the median; half were longer, half shorter). The rate of major cardiovascular events was the same in takers and non-takers. Major cardiovascular events HR 1.01 (HR is the hazard ratio; 1 means the two sides are the same). 95% CI 0.91–1.10 is the confidence range of this number. Death was the same: all-cause mortality HR 0.94 (0.88–1.02). The US Preventive Services Task Force (USPSTF), the American body that grades preventive measures, in 2022 pooled 9 randomized controlled trials and 51,550 people and found no association between multivitamins and total mortality. Grade I, meaning the evidence is insufficient and inconclusive. It looks cost-effective because "one pill covers all nutrients" sounds reasonable, and it costs only a few cents a day
- Evidence grade: A
- Source: Sesso HD et al. (2012). Multivitamins in the prevention of cardiovascular disease in men: the Physicians' Health Study II randomized controlled trial. JAMA. <https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2012.14805>; US Preventive Services Task Force (2022). Vitamin, Mineral, and Multivitamin Supplementation to Prevent Cardiovascular Disease and Cancer. JAMA. <https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2022.8970>; Gaziano JM et al. (2012). Multivitamins in the Prevention of Cancer in Men. JAMA. <https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2012.14641> (the cancer result mentioned in the Note)
- Note: Controversy: the same cohort of male physicians was also examined for cancer. Takers had about 8% lower cancer incidence (0.86–0.998, P=0.04, just barely crossing the "unlikely to be chance" threshold). Cancer mortality was about 12% lower (0.77–1.01). This range crosses 1, so it may be chance. The benefit is small, and it has only been seen in male physicians. People confirmed to be nutrient-deficient, pregnant women (taking folic acid), and people with extremely restricted diets are not covered here; supplement per medical advice

### 2. Don't take ordinary fish oil for cardiovascular disease prevention
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=中 口径=金钱 -->
- Cost: About 1 to 3 yuan a day, 400 to 1000 yuan a year. Taking it costs no time.
- Plain-language summary: Two large trials enrolled 26,000 people and 15,000 people with diabetes respectively. Those who took 1 gram of fish oil a day had the same rate of major cardiovascular events and death as those who didn't. "Fish is good for you" does not equal "fish oil capsules are good for you." Not buying saves 400 to 1,000 yuan a year.
- Benefit: The VITAL trial enrolled 25,871 people, took 1 gram of fish oil a day, and followed them for 5.3 years (median). Major cardiovascular events and death showed no difference between the two arms. Major cardiovascular events HR 0.92 (HR is the hazard ratio; 1 means the two sides are the same). 95% CI 0.80–1.06 is the confidence range of this number. It crosses 1, indicating no detectable difference. All-cause mortality HR 1.02 (0.90–1.15). The ASCEND trial enrolled 15,480 people with diabetes, also 1 gram a day, mean follow-up 7.4 years, again no difference. Serious vascular events RR 0.97 (0.87–1.08). All-cause mortality RR 0.95 (0.86–1.05). It looks cost-effective because many people take "fish is good for you" directly as "fish oil capsules are good for you"
- Evidence grade: A
- Source: Manson JE et al. (2019). Marine n-3 Fatty Acids and Prevention of Cardiovascular Disease and Cancer. NEJM. <https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa1811403>; ASCEND Study Collaborative Group (2018). Effects of n-3 Fatty Acid Supplements in Diabetes Mellitus. NEJM. <https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa1804989>; Bhatt DL et al. (2019). Cardiovascular Risk Reduction with Icosapent Ethyl for Hypertriglyceridemia. New England Journal of Medicine. <https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa1812792> (the high-purity prescription fish oil trial mentioned in the Note)
- Note: Controversy: one trial was effective. The REDUCE-IT trial enrolled 8,179 people who already had cardiovascular disease or diabetes, were all on statins, and had triglycerides still high (135–499 mg/dL). They took 4 g of pure EPA prescription drug daily; event rate 17.2% vs 22.0%. Risk about a quarter lower (HR 0.75, 0.68–0.83). But that is a high-dose prescription drug for high-risk patients, not equivalent to ordinary people taking supplement-grade fish oil. What's covered here also does not include eating fish itself

### 3. Don't give vitamin D supplements to people who aren't deficient, for longevity
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=中 口径=金钱 -->
- Cost: About 0.2 to 0.5 yuan a day, 100 to 200 yuan a year. The money isn't much; the real cost is the illusion that you've done prevention.
- Plain-language summary: People who were not deficient to begin with, given 5 years of supplementation, had the same rate of cancer, cardiovascular disease, and death as those not supplemented. People confirmed to be deficient, those with osteoporosis, and those with little sun exposure are not covered by this entry; they supplement per medical advice.
- Benefit: The VITAL trial enrolled 25,871 people, took 2000 IU a day, followed them for 5.3 years (median). Cancer, cardiovascular events, and death showed no difference. Invasive cancer HR 0.96 (HR is the hazard ratio; 1 means the two sides are the same; 95% CI 0.88–1.06 is the confidence range). Major cardiovascular events HR 0.97 (0.85–1.12). All-cause mortality HR 0.99 (0.87–1.12). The D-Health trial enrolled 21,315 Australians aged 60+. They took 60,000 IU monthly and were followed for 5 years. This trial too showed no difference: all-cause mortality HR 1.04 (0.93–1.18). Mortality rate was 5.3% vs 5.1%. It looks cost-effective because some observational studies — which only track records without intervention — found people with low vitamin D died more, and this was misread as "supplementing helps"
- Evidence grade: A
- Source: Manson JE et al. (2019). Vitamin D Supplements and Prevention of Cancer and Cardiovascular Disease. NEJM. <https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa1809944>; Neale RE et al. (2022). The D-Health Trial: a randomised controlled trial of the effect of vitamin D on mortality. Lancet Diabetes Endocrinol. <https://doi.org/10.1016/S2213-8587(21)00345-4>
- Note: Controversy: neither of these trials specifically recruited people who were genuinely vitamin-D-deficient, so the conclusions apply only to "people not deficient to begin with." Those confirmed deficient, those with osteoporosis, those with little sun exposure, and infants should still supplement per medical advice. This entry only looked at total mortality, cancer, and cardiovascular events; other outcomes were not examined

### 4. Don't take antioxidant supplements (β-carotene, vitamin E, vitamin A) for cancer prevention
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=中 口径=死亡率 -->
- Cost: About 0.5 to 2 yuan a day. For smokers, the real cost is that lung cancer risk goes up.
- Plain-language summary: This entry is more than just "no use." Pool 78 trials and nearly 300,000 people, and those taking antioxidant supplements had slightly higher mortality. In two large trials done specifically on smokers, β-carotene raised lung cancer incidence by 18% to 28% and death by 8% to 17%. Smokers and people exposed to asbestos in particular should not take them.
- Benefit: Cochrane pooled 78 randomized controlled trials and 296,707 people. Mortality in those taking antioxidant supplements was RR 1.02 (RR is the risk ratio; 1 means the two sides are the same; 95% CI 0.98–1.05 is the confidence range). Looking only at the trials with more rigorous design less prone to bias, RR 1.04 (1.01–1.07), slightly higher. β-carotene alone RR 1.05 (1.01–1.09), vitamin E alone RR 1.03 (1.00–1.05). The ATBC trial enrolled 29,133 male smokers, took 20 mg a day, lung cancer incidence 18% higher (3%–36%), total mortality 8% higher (1%–16%). The CARET trial enrolled 18,314 smokers or people exposed to asbestos. Their lung cancer risk was about 28% higher (RR 1.28, 1.04–1.57). Mortality risk about 17% higher (all-cause mortality RR 1.17, 1.03–1.33). It looks cost-effective because the chain "antioxidant means anti-aging, anti-aging prevents cancer" sounds at every step like common sense
- Evidence grade: A
- Source: Bjelakovic G et al. (2012). Antioxidant supplements for prevention of mortality in healthy participants and patients with various diseases. Cochrane Database Syst Rev. <https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD007176.pub2>; The Alpha-Tocopherol, Beta Carotene Cancer Prevention Study Group (1994). The effect of vitamin E and beta carotene on the incidence of lung cancer and other cancers in male smokers. NEJM. <https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJM199404143301501>; Omenn GS et al. (1996). Effects of a combination of beta carotene and vitamin A on lung cancer and cardiovascular disease. NEJM. <https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJM199605023341802>
- Note: The US body that grades preventive measures, USPSTF, in 2022 gave β-carotene and vitamin E a D grade, meaning it recommends against use. Smokers and people exposed to asbestos have already been harmed. Antioxidants naturally contained in vegetables and fruits are not covered here; eat as usual

### 5. Don't count on glucosamine/chondroitin to treat knee osteoarthritis
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=中 口径=金钱 -->
- Cost: About 2 to 5 yuan a day, 700 to 1800 yuan a year.
- Plain-language summary: People with knee osteoarthritis who took placebo for 24 weeks also had 60% reporting less pain. Those taking glucosamine or chondroitin were only 4 to 5 percentage points higher, small enough to be chance. Spending 700 to 1,800 yuan a year essentially buys that 60% placebo effect.
- Benefit: The GAIT trial enrolled 1,583 knee osteoarthritis patients, treated for 24 weeks. Among those taking placebo, 60.1% reported pain relief. Glucosamine was only 3.9 percentage points higher (P=0.30). Chondroitin 5.3 percentage points higher (P=0.17). The two combined 6.5 percentage points higher (P=0.09). These differences are all too small to rule out chance. The same trial's active comparator celecoxib was 10.0 percentage points higher (P=0.008). It looks cost-effective because "the raw material for cartilage" sounds like it targets the symptom, and just with placebo 60% report it works
- Evidence grade: A
- Source: Clegg DO et al. (2006). Glucosamine, chondroitin sulfate, and the two in combination for painful knee osteoarthritis. NEJM. <https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa052771>
- Note: Controversy: in the same trial, the subgroup that was in severe pain at baseline had an effective rate of 79.2% on the combination vs 54.3% in the control (P=0.002). But the authors say this was dug out of the data after the fact and can only serve as a clue, not a conclusion. The evidence-based approaches for knee osteoarthritis are weight loss and muscle training, see Section 2

### 6. Don't rely on vitamin C to prevent colds
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=小 口径=金钱 -->
- Cost: One effervescent tablet about 1 to 2 yuan, 300 to 700 yuan a year.
- Plain-language summary: Taking vitamin C daily basically doesn't reduce how often you catch a cold; starting it after a cold hits has inconsistent effects. Daily use shortens a cold by about 8%, i.e., about half a day less misery; whether that's worth 300 to 500 yuan a year is for you to compute. Marathon runners and soldiers marching in cold regions are the exception — they had about half as many colds.
- Benefit: Cochrane pooled 29 controlled trials and 11,306 people. In ordinary people, daily vitamin C basically does not reduce the number of colds. Cold incidence RR 0.97 (RR is the risk ratio; 1 means the two sides are the same). 95% CI 0.94–1.00 is the confidence range. For trials that started vitamin C after the cold, the original text says "no consistent effect": some trials had effect, some didn't. In the same review, long-term daily use shortened cold duration by about 8% (adults, 95% CI 3%–12%), and by 14% in children (7%–21%). Marathon runners, skiers, and soldiers marching in cold regions are exceptions. In 5 trials with 598 people, colds were about half as frequent. Incidence RR 0.48 (95% CI 0.35–0.64). It looks cost-effective because it's cheap, sweet and pleasant to drink, and "vitamin C prevents colds" has been circulating for half a century
- Evidence grade: A
- Source: Hemilä H, Chalker E (2013). Vitamin C for preventing and treating the common cold. Cochrane Database Syst Rev. <https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD000980.pub4>
- Note: Controversy: in the same review, daily use did shorten cold duration. Adults about 8% (3%–12%), children 14% (7%–21%). That works out to about half a day less illness per cold. Marathon runners, skiers, soldiers marching in cold regions are exceptions; in 5 trials with 598 people, colds were about half as frequent (RR 0.48, 0.35–0.64). The overwhelming majority of people are not in these two categories

### 7. Don't give yourself a "whole-body PET-CT" or "tumor marker package" when you have no symptoms
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=中 口径=死亡率 -->
- Cost: Whole-body PET-CT runs about 7,000 to 10,000 yuan per scan, plus a dose of ionizing radiation. Tumor marker package a few hundred yuan. The real cost comes afterward: an "abnormality" turns out to be a false alarm (false positive), followed by repeat tests, tissue sampling, surgery — the money and suffering lie here.
- Plain-language summary: When asymptomatic people go in for screening, what's found most often is a false alarm. More than 80% of those who had whole-body CT had at least one "abnormality," nearly 40% were called back for follow-up, yet most were benign. Women screened for ovarian cancer markers had slightly higher ovarian cancer mortality, and some had unnecessary surgery because of a false alarm.
- Benefit: The US body that grades preventive measures, USPSTF, gave screening for ovarian cancer with CA-125 and ultrasound in asymptomatic women a D grade, meaning it recommends against. The basis is the PLCO trial. Ovarian cancer mortality: screening group 0.34%, usual-care group 0.29%. RR 1.18 (95% CI 0.82–1.71). Screening brought no benefit. The rate of unnecessary surgery due to a false positive, across trials, was 0.2%–3.25%, and among these up to 15% had serious surgical complications. Another study gave 1,192 asymptomatic people whole-body CT screening. 86% had at least one "abnormality" detected, 37% were recommended for further work-up. The original text says "most findings were described as benign." It looks cost-effective because "one scan covers the whole body, find it early, treat it early" fits intuition too well
- Evidence grade: A
- Source: US Preventive Services Task Force (2018). Screening for Ovarian Cancer: US Preventive Services Task Force Recommendation Statement. JAMA (the same recommendation also has a USPSTF official page). <https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2017.21926>, <https://www.uspreventiveservicestaskforce.org/uspstf/recommendation/ovarian-cancer-screening>; Furtado CD et al. (2005). Whole-body CT screening: spectrum of findings and recommendations in 1192 patients. Radiology. <https://doi.org/10.1148/radiol.2372041741>
- Note: The tumor-marker part is backed by a randomized controlled trial. The whole-body CT and PET-CT part has only "what happens to people who get one" tracking records, no controlled comparison; the evidence is one tier weaker, equivalent to grade B. The genuinely evidence-backed screenings, like colorectal cancer, cervical cancer, breast cancer, and low-dose chest CT for high-risk people, are a different matter, see Section 1. People who already have symptoms, or people already diagnosed with cancer doing PET-CT, that's diagnostic care, not screening, and is a different matter

### 8. Don't count on wristband/watch exercise tracking to help you lose weight
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=中 口径=金钱 -->
- Cost: The device runs 200 to 2,000 yuan. Plus the daily attention you spend looking at the data.
- Plain-language summary: Overweight people on the same diet and exercise plan, those who wore a fitness tracker lost 3.5 kg over two years; those who didn't actually lost 5.9 kg. To lose weight, paying for a device doesn't help; what works is the exercise itself.
- Benefit: The IDEA trial enrolled 471 overweight or obese adults and followed them for 24 months. Everyone did the same diet and exercise plan; the only difference was whether they wore a wearable device. The device group lost 3.5 kg (95% CI 2.6–4.5, this is the confidence range). The non-device group lost 5.9 kg (5.0–6.8), actually more. The two groups differed by 2.4 kg (1.0–3.7, P=0.002). It looks cost-effective because "what gets measured gets managed" sounds reasonable, and a device is a one-time purchase
- Evidence grade: B
- Source: Jakicic JM et al. (2016). Effect of Wearable Technology Combined With a Lifestyle Intervention on Long-term Weight Loss: The IDEA Randomized Clinical Trial. JAMA. <https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2016.12858>
- Note: This is a single trial, using an arm-worn device from the early 2010s; you can't conclude from it that "all wearables are useless." Exercise itself is effective, see Section 2. The conclusion is one sentence: to lose weight, a device is not a necessary expense

### 9. Don't pay a premium for organic food to be "healthier"
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=中 口径=金钱 -->
- Cost: Usually 1 to 3 times the price of the equivalent conventional food.
- Plain-language summary: There's no strong evidence that organic food is more nutritious. Studies that actually looked at human health outcomes after eating it number only 3, and none found a difference. Organic produce does have a 30-percentage-point lower detection rate of pesticide residues, but detection does not equal exceeding limits. Eating more fruits and vegetables itself helps; whether they're organic doesn't change that.
- Benefit: A systematic review combined 17 studies done in humans and 223 studies measuring food composition. The conclusion was "lack of strong evidence that organic foods are significantly more nutritious than conventional foods." Only 3 of these studies looked at human health outcomes (looking at allergies and symptomatic infections), and no difference was seen between the two. Organic produce had a 30-percentage-point lower detection rate of pesticide residues, but detection does not equal exceeding limits. It looks cost-effective because the chain "no pesticides means safer, safer means healthier" sounds at every step like common sense
- Evidence grade: B
- Source: Smith-Spangler C et al. (2012). Are organic foods safer or healthier than conventional alternatives?: a systematic review. Ann Intern Med. <https://doi.org/10.7326/0003-4819-157-5-201209040-00007>
- Note: Studies that directly examine human health outcomes are very few. So the conclusion is "there's no evidence it's better," not "it's been proven to have no benefit." Environmental impact, animal welfare, and taste are not addressed here. Eating more fruits and vegetables itself works; whether they're organic doesn't change that

### 10. Don't spend big money on health supplements, herbal pastes, and tonics to "regulate your body"
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=大 口径=金钱 -->
- Cost: From several hundred to over ten thousand yuan. Once packaged as a gift, the price often multiplies several-fold.
- Plain-language summary: The department that regulates health foods requires that the packaging print one sentence: health foods are not drugs and cannot replace drugs in treating disease. Multivitamins, fish oil, vitamin D and other common ingredients have already been examined one by one in the first six entries of this section, all showing no benefit. This money is mostly spent buying for parents and bringing it to their door; the thought is fine, it's the choice of item that's wrong.
- Benefit: The State Administration for Market Regulation requires that the front of health-food packaging dedicate no less than 20% of its area to one sentence: "Health foods are not drugs and cannot replace drugs in treating disease." The same regulation confines the use of health foods to "supplementing dietary nutrients, maintaining or improving the body's health status, or reducing risk factors for disease." The regulator itself does not recognize them as treating disease. What they most commonly contain — multivitamins, fish oil, vitamin D, antioxidants, glucosamine, vitamin C — and what each shows in randomized controlled trials, has already been listed entry by entry in the first 6 entries of this section. It looks cost-effective because "doesn't treat disease, only regulates" is a phrasing that can never be proven wrong; no one can prove it false. And the money spent on gifting rides on social obligation, not evidence
- Evidence grade: C
- Source: State Administration for Market Regulation (2019). Press conference on the "Guide to Warning Language Labeling of Health Foods" and the "Administrative Measures for the Catalog of Health Food Raw Materials and Health Functions." <https://www.samr.gov.cn/tssps/sjdt/tpxw/art/2023/art_4b658b824b1b4b0ba57c09a56cc93aad.html>
- Note: The conclusion is "no evidence supports," not "proven ineffective," so only grade C. For products whose ingredients are clearly stated, check the corresponding studies in the earlier entries. Those confirmed deficient in nutrients or being treated for an illness should follow medical advice. What's to be rejected is the product, not the sentiment. For older relatives who already bought it and are taking it, as long as they're not using it to replace their actual medication and it hasn't caused problems, there's no need to argue over it; getting their vaccines and screenings done is more important. The same money redirected to the following items has a source for each in the book. Accompany elderly relatives to get the annual flu vaccine, see Section 1, Entry 20. The shingles vaccine after 50, see Section 1, Entry 21. The pneumococcal vaccine after 65, see Section 1, Entry 22. Buy a blood pressure monitor and make sure he takes his blood pressure medication as prescribed and gets his blood pressure to target, see Section 1, Entry 7. Modify the bathroom and stairs at home, and train balance and leg strength with him, see Section 1, Entry 13. Take him to cancer screenings at the appropriate age, see Section 1, Entries 17 to 19. For elderly relatives already bedridden long-term, pressure ulcer prevention and long-term care insurance are in Section 17, Entries 7 and 8. When bringing things to their door, switch to fruit, rice and flour, things he likes to eat — more practical than herbal paste. What really needs blocking is two categories: investment-style "pay first" eldercare, and products that replace actual medication, see Section 17, Entry 5

### 11. Don't take probiotics long-term when you have no gut issues
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=小 口径=金钱 -->
- Cost: About 2 to 5 yuan a day, 700 to 1800 yuan a year.
- Plain-language summary: Pooling 45 trials in healthy adults, taking probiotics does not durably change the gut microbiome; once you stop, it returns to baseline, and blood lipids don't improve either. Specific situations like antibiotic-associated diarrhea have separate evidence and are not covered here.
- Benefit: A review pooled 45 trials done in healthy adults and concluded that "probiotic supplementation did not induce persistent changes in the gut microbiome, nor did it improve blood lipids in healthy adults." Gut microbes do change, but only temporarily; once stopped, they revert. It looks cost-effective because "gut health" is broad enough to fit anything, and a daily bottle is cheap
- Evidence grade: B
- Source: Khalesi S et al. (2019). A review of probiotic supplementation in healthy adults: helpful or hype? Eur J Clin Nutr. <https://doi.org/10.1038/s41430-018-0135-9>
- Note: The same review noted some minor improvements in healthy people, like better-formed stool of appropriate firmness, and more lactobacilli in the vagina of women. Antibiotic-associated diarrhea, and specific strains used for specific conditions, have their own separate evidence and are a different matter. This entry concerns only healthy people taking daily supplements

### 12. Don't force yourself to take cold showers to "boost immunity"
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=小 口径=金钱 -->
- Cost: Free. Each day you endure 30 to 90 seconds of discomfort; sticking with it in winter takes willpower. People with cardiovascular disease should also be careful — cold stimulation carries risks for them.
- Plain-language summary: In a trial of 3,018 people, those who took cold showers took 29% fewer sick days, but the total number of sick days was the same as others, so it's not really fewer illnesses. A 2025 pooling of 11 trials found that after a cold plunge, inflammatory markers actually rose briefly, with no change in immune markers. Take them if you like; just don't treat it as a health investment.
- Benefit: A randomized controlled trial in the Netherlands, 3,018 people, took a 30-, 60-, or 90-second cold shower after a hot shower. These people took 29% fewer sick days (IRR 0.71, this is the ratio of rates). But the original text states "no significant difference in sick days" — total days of illness were the same between arms. Quality of life, work efficiency, and anxiety also showed no meaningful difference. A 2025 systematic review pooled 11 randomized trials and 3,177 people. Immediately and 1 hour after a cold plunge, inflammatory markers actually rose. Immune markers did not change. Stress was only reduced at the 12-hour time point. The authors themselves state the evidence is "limited by few RCTs and small samples," meaning such randomized trials are too few and each is small. It looks cost-effective because it's free, and "suffering works" is too smooth an intuition
- Evidence grade: B
- Source: Buijze GA et al. (2016). The Effect of Cold Showering on Health and Work: A Randomized Controlled Trial. PLOS ONE. <https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0161749>; Cain T et al. (2025). Effects of cold-water immersion on health and wellbeing: A systematic review and meta-analysis. PLOS ONE. <https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0317615>
- Note: Controversy: the 29% fewer sick days is real and unlikely to be chance. But total days of illness didn't decrease, so it's more like "going to work despite being sick" than actually fewer illnesses. Athletes using cold water for recovery after training is another question, not addressed here. Cold showers themselves aren't harmful to healthy people; just don't treat them as a health investment

### 13. Don't buy "detox," "colon cleanse," "enzyme," or "alkaline water" products
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=中 口径=金钱 -->
- Cost: Several hundred to several thousand yuan. The kind that relies on fasting to "detox" can also push blood sugar low and leave you malnourished.
- Plain-language summary: Detox is a big industry, but the clinical evidence supporting it is very thin, and no one has seriously tested commercial detox diets in trials. Some have looked at whether dietary acid-alkali, alkaline water, and cancer are linked; only 1 study was found that met the bar, and it showed no association.
- Benefit: That review concluded "despite the detox industry's booming growth, clinical evidence supporting these diets is very thin," and "there are no randomized controlled trials of commercial detox diets." Another systematic review specifically examined whether dietary acid load, alkaline water, and cancer are linked: it screened 8,278 papers, only 1 met the bar for inclusion, and it too showed no association. The authors write that "promoting alkaline diet and alkaline water to the public for cancer prevention or treatment is unreasonable." It looks cost-effective because the premises "the body has toxins to excrete" and "acidic constitution causes cancer" sound a lot like physiology
- Evidence grade: C
- Source: Klein AV, Kiat H (2015). Detox diets for toxin elimination and weight management: a critical review of the evidence. J Hum Nutr Diet. <https://doi.org/10.1111/jhn.12286>; Fenton TR, Huang T (2016). Systematic review of the association between dietary acid load, alkaline water and cancer. BMJ Open. <https://doi.org/10.1136/bmjopen-2015-010438>
- Note: Only "no evidence supports" can be said, so only grade C. Clearing the waste products of metabolism is exactly what the liver and kidneys do. As long as these two organs aren't diseased, no extra "detox" is needed

### 14. Don't force yourself to drink water to hit "8 glasses a day"
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=小 口径=金钱 -->
- Cost: Free. It takes some time and attention to remember to drink, and nighttime urination increases.
- Plain-language summary: Someone specifically tracked down the origin of "8 glasses a day" and found no supporting study. Food and other beverages already contain a lot of water, so you don't need to drink that much. Drink when thirsty and check the color of your urine — that's enough. Those working in heat, doing strenuous exercise, those who've had kidney stones, and older people who don't feel thirsty much should drink proactively.

### 22. Don't buy crystals, bead bracelets, or pixiu-type objects to "change your luck," "attract wealth," or "nourish you"
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=些 收益=中 口径=金钱 -->
- Cost: Free. All you do is not include "changing luck" or "nourishing" in the reasons you buy it. The hard part is not following when everyone around you is wearing them and saying they work.
- Plain-language summary: That wearing them changes luck, attracts wealth, or nourishes you has no verifiable evidence. It's the same mechanism as why fortune-telling seems accurate: the wording is vague enough that you fit it to yourself. When sellers push into "treats disease" territory, that's false advertising; in 2026 a livestream was busted for this. Buying as decoration is fine.
- Benefit: Classic classroom experiment shows that everyone received the same vague "personal analysis," yet generally found it accurate — the phenomenon is called the Barnum effect. It explains where the experience of "things really went smoother after I put it on" comes from. That author's conclusion was "being accepted by the subject does not prove the interpretation is correct." Official case: the State Administration for Market Regulation on June 4, 2026 published a batch of typical cases of "anti-counterfeiting and source-cleaning" in the traditional crafts market. Among them, a jewelry store in Sihui, Guangdong, fabricated product materials in its livestream. It also falsely claimed the products had effects like "preventing and treating rheumatism, boosting immunity, improving sleep quality, and clearing blood vessels." The store used scripted acting to mislead and deceive consumers. This violated Article 9, paragraph 1 of the Anti-Unfair Competition Law (nationwide)
- Evidence grade: B
- Source: Forer BR (1949). The fallacy of personal validation: A classroom demonstration of gullibility. Journal of Abnormal and Social Psychology. <https://doi.org/10.1037/h0059240>; State Administration for Market Regulation (2026). The State Administration for Market Regulation publishes a batch of typical cases of "anti-counterfeiting and source-cleaning" in the traditional crafts market. <https://www.samr.gov.cn/xw/zj/art/2026/art_1529399cfe874b8780c4496eb0158020.html>
- Note: The conclusion is one sentence only: its effects are unverifiable, and no predictive power can be measured. No belief or custom is evaluated; gifting, commemoration, heirloom purposes are also not in scope. Two things to distinguish: the effects sellers claim have explicit regulations to rely on and can be complained about; whereas claims like "changing luck" have neither research nor a way to be proven false, and can only be controlled by your own budget. Before buying, first treat it as an ordinary ornament, and ask yourself "if it changes nothing, would I still pay this price." How to check materials and certificates, and why treating it as an investment doesn't pay off, see Section 5, Entries 33 and 34 (bead bracelets and luxury watches counted by money spent; jewelry and jade require CMA reports). Spending on fortune-telling see Entry 15 of this section (fortune-telling, tarot, astrology)

### 23. Don't count on shopping to improve your mood or sense of identity
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=是 收益=中 口径=金钱 -->
- Cost: Free. The hard part is holding back from placing an order when you're feeling low.
- Plain-language summary: The more weight people place on money and material things, the lower their self-reported happiness — but the association is weak, and you can't tell which causes which. Other research has found that spending money on an experience brings more happiness than buying a thing. So when you're down, spending on an outing or a meal with someone is more likely to help than filling a shopping cart.
- Benefit: A meta-analysis (pooling many studies together) had 259 independent samples and 753 effect sizes. The result: the more people value material things, the lower their self-reported well-being. On the most commonly used multidimensional scale, the correlation coefficient r = -0.19 (adjusted ρ = -0.24). On scales that measure only the "valuing money" aspect, the association is even weaker, r = -0.08 to -0.11. By type of outcome, the strength varies. The strongest associations were with risky health and consumer behaviors and negative self-evaluations, r = -0.28 to -0.44. The weakest were with life satisfaction and negative emotions, r = -0.13 to -0.15. The authors also did mediation analysis (checking what the association passes through), suggesting this negative association may be because psychological needs aren't being met. Another study: in two surveys of different populations, respondents said money spent on acquiring a life experience made them happier than buying things. In subsequent lab experiments, recalling a purchase made for an experience produced a better mood than recalling a purchase made for a thing (nationwide metric doesn't apply; both studies used foreign samples)
- Evidence grade: B
- Source: Dittmar H, Bond R, Hurst M, Kasser T (2014). The relationship between materialism and personal well-being: A meta-analysis. Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 107(5), 879-924. <https://doi.org/10.1037/a0037409>; Van Boven L, Gilovich T (2003). To do or to have? That is the question. Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 85(6), 1193-1202. <https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.85.6.1193>
- Note: Controversy: most included studies surveyed one group at one time point, looking at whether two things co-occur, and can't distinguish which came first. The authors themselves note in the conclusion that clarifying direction requires more experiments and longitudinal studies. So it can't be read as "shopping makes people unhappy," only as "counting on shopping to become happy holds little promise." And the association is weak: r = -0.19 explains only a few percentage points of variance, and people differ a lot. Both studies used foreign samples; no equivalent citable data exist for Chinese populations. This entry doesn't oppose spending money on things you like; it opposes treating shopping as a way to regulate mood, because that trains your first response to a bad mood to be placing an order again. For specific money-saving actions, see Section 5. For evidence-based approaches when mood is low, see the second half of Section 22 and Section 29. For budgets added "to be a tier above," see Entry 24 of this section (moving up a tier)

### 24. Don't spend more to trade up housing, cars, or social circles to "move up a tier among those around you"
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=些 收益=中 口径=金钱 -->
- Cost: Free. All you do is ask, before adding to the budget, one more question: is this premium buying the thing itself, or my position in this group? The hard part is not following when everyone around you has traded up.
- Plain-language summary: Comparing people with the same income, the more a neighbor earns, the lower this person's reported happiness. This happens only among neighbors with the same education who interact frequently. So the extra money spent to move into a place where everyone is better off than you buys "moving yourself down a tier." But when incomes rise together, people still feel better — absolute level still counts.
- Benefit: The US National Survey of Families and Households (NSFH) re-interviewed the same cohort in two rounds. The authors matched this data to local mean income. Local mean income (PUMA) was computed from the 1990 census 5% sample. After removing the effects of own income and a series of personal characteristics, the result was: the higher neighbors' income, the lower self-reported happiness. The effect is large, robust to alternative specifications, and highly significant. The authors' wording is that the decline in happiness from "neighbors' income rising" is of the same order of magnitude as from "own income falling by the same amount." When switched to within-person comparison (called adding individual fixed effects), the coefficient's direction and magnitude were similar, only significance dropped to the 10% level. Further restricted to comparisons within the same state and the same survey round (called state and survey-round fixed effects), results were essentially unchanged. This means it's not driven by "people who like moving to poorer areas being happier to begin with," nor by unmeasured state-level factors. The effect is stronger among those who frequently interact with neighbors, and not significant among those whose friends live outside the community. Renters and homeowners showed no difference — this does not support the "expensive areas have high housing prices, so less actual disposable income" explanation. Happiness declines only with neighbors of the same education, and is essentially unresponsive to neighbors of different education. And it is transmitted mainly through "satisfaction with one's own material circumstances," not through satisfaction with family life or other aspects. Finally, if own and neighbors' income rise by the same proportion, happiness still rises
- Evidence grade: B
- Source: Luttmer EFP (2005). Neighbors as Negatives: Relative Earnings and Well-Being. The Quarterly Journal of Economics, 120(3), 963–1002. <https://doi.org/10.1093/qje/120.3.963>; Luttmer EFP (2004). Neighbors as Negatives: Relative Earnings and Well-Being. NBER Working Paper No. 10667 (the wording and magnitudes in this entry were verified verbatim against this version). <https://www.nber.org/papers/w10667>
- Note: Reasons for grading B: data come from repeated re-interviews with the same cohort, this is a single study, and the sample is foreign; the outcome is self-reported happiness, not money. The author did use statistical methods to remove several classes of confounding, like same-person comparison over time and same-state same-round comparison, but it's not a randomized trial after all. No citable equivalent data exist for Chinese populations either. The benefit magnitude is graded "moderate," following the metric of Entry 23 of this section (don't count on shopping to improve mood). The money saved could reach the 10,000-yuan level. But "how much happiness that saved money buys" has no numerical support, so it isn't graded "large" at the 10,000-yuan level. This entry doesn't oppose spending on a bigger home, a quieter neighborhood, a shorter commute — those buy the thing itself. What to block is the one reason "my colleagues all traded up, I can't be worse than them." For how to rank where to live, see Section 4, Entry 18 (when choosing housing, put commute time first). For shopping to regulate mood, see Entry 23 of this section (don't count on shopping to improve mood). For constantly comparing upward online, see Section 3, Entry 21 (don't make "how others are doing" required daily reading)

### 25. Don't believe "willpower is like a muscle that gets used up"
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=中 口径=时间 -->
- Cost: Free. What changes is just one claim.
- Plain-language summary: The popular claim: do one self-control task first, and you're more likely to slip on the next, so use willpower sparingly. 23 labs and 2,141 people re-ran this experiment with the same protocol; the result was essentially zero. Stop explaining yourself with "my willpower ran out today," and don't pay for courses teaching you to stockpile willpower.
- Benefit: A multi-lab preregistered replication, 23 labs and 2,141 participants total, re-ran the sequential-task paradigm per the standardized protocol of Sripada et al.: do one self-control-depleting task, then a second task requiring self-control. The pooled result showed a very small ego-depletion effect, and the 95% confidence interval included 0 (d = 0.04, 95% CI −0.07 to 0.15). The authors discussed whether the "self-control as a limited resource" model still holds
- Evidence grade: A
- Source: Hagger MS, Chatzisarantis NLD, Alberts H, et al. (2016). A Multilab Preregistered Replication of the Ego-Depletion Effect. Perspectives on Psychological Science, 11(4), 546–573. <https://doi.org/10.1177/1745691616652873>
- Note: What's to be rejected is the "self-control as a depletable resource" model, not the existence of fatigue. Sleep deprivation and prolonged work reduce efficiency — that's a different matter, handled by the sleep and working-hours entries in Section 3. What has randomized-trial support is changing the environment and changing how you write things down, see Section 4, Entry 10 (move unwanted things farther away) and Section 4, Entry 1 (write "at what time, where, on encountering what, do what"). The other entries in this section count money; this one counts time and energy, and the two aren't converted

### 26. Don't count on eating breakfast or 16:8 intermittent fasting to control your weight; pick a meal schedule you can sustain long-term
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=小 口径=死亡率 -->
- Cost: Free. What's saved is the daily mental effort of wrestling with meal timing.
- Plain-language summary: The popular claim is that skipping breakfast leads to overeating at lunch and dinner, with higher total daily intake. The trials say otherwise: people who skipped breakfast ate 200+ fewer kcal a day and weighed slightly less. 16:8 vs simply eating less showed no weight difference over a year. Long-term breakfast skippers do have about 17% higher cardiovascular disease, but total mortality shows no difference.
- Benefit: First, the claim "skipping breakfast makes you eat more later." A meta-analysis pooled 13 randomized trials. People assigned to eat breakfast consumed 259.79 kcal more per day than those assigned to skip (95% CI 78.87–440.71, this is the confidence range). Weight was 0.44 kg higher (0.07–0.82). Breakfast skippers did not make up for it at lunch and dinner. These trials had short follow-up — weight trials averaged 7 weeks, intake trials 2 weeks. Next, 16:8-style eating only within fixed hours. One trial had 116 overweight adults do 12 weeks. The group eating only from 12 noon to 8 pm and the group eating three regular meals differed in weight by −0.26 kg (−1.30 to 0.78), not significant. Another in Guangzhou, 139 obese patients, followed 12 months. Time-restricted eating plus calorie restriction lost 1.8 kg more than calorie restriction alone (−4.0 to 0.4), also not significant. Finally, whether long-term breakfast skipping is harmful. In a pooling of 2.38 million people, breakfast skippers had about 17% higher cardiovascular disease risk (OR 1.17, 1.09–1.26), and about 49% higher cardiovascular mortality (OR 1.49, 1.20–1.84). But these are observational studies without randomized grouping. In the original 17-to-23-year follow-up study, the all-cause mortality hazard ratio was 1.19, confidence range 0.99 to 1.42, crossing 1, not qualifying as a difference
- Evidence grade: A
- Source: Sievert K, Hussain SM, Page MJ, et al. (2019). Effect of breakfast on weight and energy intake: systematic review and meta-analysis of randomised controlled trials. BMJ. <https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj.l42>; Lowe DA, Wu N, Rohdin-Bibby L, et al. (2020). Effects of Time-Restricted Eating on Weight Loss and Other Metabolic Parameters in Women and Men With Overweight and Obesity. JAMA Internal Medicine. <https://doi.org/10.1001/jamainternmed.2020.4153>; Liu D, Huang Y, Huang C, et al. (2022). Calorie Restriction with or without Time-Restricted Eating in Weight Loss. New England Journal of Medicine. <https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa2114833>; Zhang H, Zhang S, Liu Y, et al. (2025). The association between skipping breakfast and cardiovascular disease: a meta analysis. Frontiers in Cardiovascular Medicine. <https://doi.org/10.3389/fcvm.2025.1565806>; Rong S, Snetselaar LG, Xu G, et al. (2019). Association of Skipping Breakfast With Cardiovascular and All-Cause Mortality. Journal of the American College of Cardiology. <https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jacc.2019.01.065>; Fernandes-Alves D, Teixeira GP, Guimarães KC, Crispim CA (2026). Systematic Review and Meta-analysis of Randomized Clinical Trials Comparing Time-Restricted Eating With and Without Caloric Restriction for Weight Loss. Nutrition Reviews. <https://doi.org/10.1093/nutrit/nuaf053>; Sun T, Zhang L, Lu Y, et al. (2024). Non-linear relationship between the first meal time of the day and gallstone incidence in American adults. Frontiers in Nutrition. <https://doi.org/10.3389/fnut.2024.1521707>; Liu T, Wang Y, Wang X, et al. (2023). Habitually Skipping Breakfast Is Associated with the Risk of Gastrointestinal Cancers: Evidence from the Kailuan Cohort Study. Journal of General Internal Medicine. <https://doi.org/10.1007/s11606-023-08094-7>
- Note: Controversy. The 17% cardiovascular figure comes from observational studies without randomization. Breakfast skippers are more likely to smoke, drink, be sedentary, and have lower income, and these factors can't be cleanly adjusted. So how much of that 17% is caused by breakfast itself is unclear. The all-cause mortality figure already crossed 1. 16:8 is also contested: pooling 30 trials and 1,341 people, with both groups eating the same amount, the time-restricted group lost 1.46 kg more (−2.65 to −0.26), at the cost of 0.41 kg more lean mass loss. But these trials are mostly short, and the longest, strictest 12-month trial said no difference. Long fasting also relates to the gallbladder. A cross-sectional survey of 6,547 people found that each hour later of the first meal of the day was associated with about 5% higher gallstone detection (OR 1.05, 1.02–1.08); those whose first meal was between 9 am and 2 pm had about 49% higher than earlier eaters (OR 1.49, 1.24–1.77). The mechanism is that bile stored longer in the gallbladder is more likely to precipitate stones. But this is a same-time-point survey; it could also be that gallbladder problems came first and changed the eating habit. The Chinese Kailuan cohort followed for 5.6 years, 369 gastrointestinal cancers: breakfast skippers had 2.32 times the colorectal cancer risk (1.34–4.01), and 5.43 times the gallbladder and extrahepatic bile duct cancer risk (1.34–21.93). The confidence range of the latter goes up to 21.93, indicating very few events — only suggestive. Taken together, for weight control alone, meal timing has little effect; don't spend mental energy on it. As for whether long-term total breakfast skipping is good or bad, existing evidence is all associative, can't prove causation, but the direction is consistently adverse. If you want to be safe, eat normally; just don't count on it to help you lose weight. The main causes of gastritis and gastric ulcer are not fasting but Helicobacter pylori and long-term painkiller use — get tested as in Section 1, Entry 23 (test for H. pylori, eradicate if positive). What to do when a checkup finds gallstones but you've never had pain, see Entry 20 of this section (don't preventively remove the gallbladder for asymptomatic gallstones). For weight control, what's actually evidence-backed is what and how much you eat, see Section 2, Entry 28 (eat 5 servings of fruits and vegetables a day), Section 2, Entry 29 (eat fewer ultra-processed foods), and Section 2, Entry 33 (keep BMI in 20–25). People with diabetes, those on glucose-lowering medication, or pregnant women are not covered; skipping a meal could cause hypoglycemia — follow medical advice. Extreme fasting and induced vomiting are a different matter, see Section 28, Entry 1 (don't use extreme dieting, fasting, or induced vomiting for weight control)
